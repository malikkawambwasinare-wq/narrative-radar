# The telling, not the events — working notes

Partial. The neuroscience brief was cut short when we wrapped the session; these are the
verified pieces worth keeping, from the narrative-identity and discourse-structure strand.
Sources checked against primary texts unless marked otherwise.

## Evaluation is the part of a telling that carries the teller

Labov's six components of a personal narrative (Labov 1972, pp. 354–396): abstract,
orientation, complicating action, **evaluation**, resolution, coda. Evaluation is the "so
what" — the clauses that say why the story was worth telling. Labov: "Departures from the
basic narrative syntax have a marked evaluative force" (p. 378).

Its devices are separable and codable: **external** (the teller steps out and says the
point), **embedded** (the point sits inside the story as reported speech or thought), and
**evaluative action** (what characters do carries it). Plus four syntactic families:
intensifiers, comparators, correlatives, explicatives.

**Why this matters for us.** Two videos can report the same events and differ entirely in
evaluation. That difference is the narrative. It is also measurable from a transcript, which
is one more reason transcripts are the gate.

In the one coding tradition that quantified Labov — Peterson & McCabe's high-point analysis,
96 children, ~1,000 narratives — the structural types are *defined* by where evaluation sits
and whether it is there at all. The developmental sequence is children learning to add it.

## Labov 2013: reportability and credibility pull against each other

A teller maximises reportability and credibility at once, and they are inversely related:
the more reportable an event, the harder it is to believe. Reportability is raised by
irrealis predicates (what might have happened); credibility by realis predicates and cited
witnesses. He treats the assignment of **praise and blame** as a central narrative function
that shapes which events are selected at all.

**For us.** That is the clock-and-villain pattern in one sentence, from linguistics rather
than psychology, and it predicts the rhetoric our lexicon already filters.

## Narrative identity: the evidence is real but modest

McAdams's redemption and contamination sequences correlate with well-being at r ≈ .37 and
−.40 (McAdams et al. 2001, n = 74). The field's own review of 30 studies (Adler et al. 2016)
says every coefficient sits "below .50 or just slightly above", and that evidence for
*structural* aspects like coherence is "largely absent". Adler 2012 found coherence had no
relation to mental health at all (r = −.02).

Redemption is culturally specific: it predicts well-being in US and Israeli samples, weakly
or not at all in Japan and Denmark (Turner et al. 2024, N = 438). A preregistered study
(N = 1,872) shows Americans prefer redemptive stories *and* their tellers.

**For us.** Do not treat a narrative's shape as universal. What reads as a compelling arc in
one audience is not one in another.

## Two cautions

- Bruner's narrative versus paradigmatic modes has **no direct empirical test**. It is a
  productive taxonomy, not a validated dual-process model. Do not cite it as a finding.
- Labovian evaluation has a known scope problem: almost any element can function
  evaluatively, and it is hard to apply consistently. If we code it, we need a narrow
  operational definition and an agreement check, not the concept as written.
