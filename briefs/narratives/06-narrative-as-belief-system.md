# Research — What a narrative is, as a belief system, across the fields that study belief

Researcher brief, 2026-09-19. Every source below was opened: full text pulled for the crux documents (Entman 1993, the NPF chapter, Treadway & McCloskey 1987, Bower/Black/Turner 1979, Bergman & Roediger 1999, Kazemi et al. 2021, Goertzel 1994, Das et al. 2026). Quoted phrases are the author's exact words and are kept short; everything else is paraphrase.

Purpose: to decide, repeatedly and defensibly, whether two sets of videos are **one narrative**, **a mutation of one**, or **two narratives**. This is a boundary problem, not a storytelling problem. Seven fields have answers. They do not agree, and the disagreements are informative.

---

## The one structural fact to hold onto

Every field below puts narrative identity at a **middle** tier, and every field gets into trouble when it drifts up or down from it.

```
TOPIC        "immigration", "the dollar"            too coarse — no outcome, nothing falsifiable
  └─ NARRATIVE   agent → mechanism → target → outcome    the tier we want
       └─ CLAIM      one checkable proposition            too fine — a narrative holds dozens
```

Fact-checkers live one tier below us. Topic modellers and "the hero's journey" live one or two tiers above. Both are real research literatures; neither is answering our question. Entman saw this in 1993: news texts can be **homogeneous at one level of analysis and competing at another** (p. 55, on pre-war Iraq coverage — a shared problem definition, contested remedies). The level must be chosen and then held.

## 1. Framing and political communication

### Entman 1993 — the four functions

Entman, R. M. (1993). Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm. *Journal of Communication* 43(4), 51–58. Read in full.

The definition, p. 52 (Entman's own italics):

> "To frame is to **select** some aspects of a perceived reality and make them more **salient** in a communicating text, in such a way as to promote a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation, and/or treatment recommendation."

The four functions, p. 52 — frames **define problems**, **diagnose causes**, **make moral judgments**, **suggest remedies**.

Three qualifications that matter for us and are usually dropped when this is cited:

- **A frame need not have all four.** Entman: "a frame in any particular text may not necessarily include all four functions" (p. 52). So a missing remedy does not make it a different frame.
- **The frame is not in the sentence.** A single sentence may do several functions and most sentences do none (p. 52). The frame is a property of the text, recovered from "the presence or absence of certain keywords, stock phrases, stereotyped images, sources of information, and sentences that provide thematically reinforcing clusters of facts or judgments" (p. 52).
- **Absence is diagnostic.** "Most frames are defined by what they omit as well as include" (p. 54). Two texts that assert the same things but differ in what they refuse to consider are, for Entman, differently framed.

Entman also locates frames in four places — communicator, text, receiver, culture (p. 52) — and defines culture as the stock of commonly invoked frames. We can only observe the **text** location. Entman is explicit that text frames do not guarantee audience frames (p. 53). Our narrative labels are claims about texts, not about viewers' heads; the brief should not pretend otherwise.

**What counts as a distinct frame, for Entman:** a different configuration of the four functions. Not different wording, not different evidence, not different examples.

### Narrative Policy Framework — the formal elements and the minimum bar

Primary source read in full: Shanahan, E. A., Jones, M. D., McBeth, M. K., & Radaelli, C. M. (2018). "The Narrative Policy Framework," in Weible & Sabatier (eds), *Theories of the Policy Process*, 4th ed., pp. 173–213. Founding statement: Jones, M. D., & McBeth, M. K. (2010). A Narrative Policy Framework: Clear Enough to Be Wrong? *Policy Studies Journal* 38(2), 329–353.

NPF's four **core elements** (2018 chapter, pp. 175–176):

1. **Setting** — the policy context: legal and constitutional parameters, geography, evidence, economic conditions, norms. Stage props; usually taken for granted, sometimes themselves contested.
2. **Characters** — "Policy narratives must have at least one character" (p. 176). Victims harmed, villains who do the harm, heroes who promise relief. Later NPF work adds beneficiaries, allies/opponents, entrepreneurs.
3. **Plot** — situates characters and their relationships in time and space; the arc of action.
4. **Moral of the story** — "in the NPF, the moral of the story is often equivalent to the policy solution" (p. 176).

**The minimum definitional bar** (p. 176) — this is the crux passage, and it is much thinner than most people assume:

> "NPF scholarship has maintained a definition of a policy narrative as featuring at least one character and containing some public policy referent"

(citing Shanahan, Jones, McBeth & Lane 2013, *PSJ* 41(3):453–483, at p. 457.)

So: **one character plus a policy referent**. No plot required, no moral required, no setting required. NPF explicitly permits other definitions but demands you declare which you are using — good discipline for us.

NPF adds three **strategies** (pp. 177–178), which are content, not form:

- **Scope of conflict** (after Schattschneider 1960): losers expand the conflict by diffusing costs and concentrating benefits; winners contain it by doing the reverse.
- **Causal mechanisms**, taken from Deborah Stone's four causal theories — **intentional, inadvertent, accidental, mechanical**. This is load-bearing for us; see §The Test.
- **Devil–angel shift**: measured as how much the narrator villainises opponents relative to how much they heroise themselves. The devil shift is quoted from Weible, Sabatier & McQueen (2009, 132–133): actors "exaggerate the malicious motives, behaviors, and influence of opponents."

### How NPF operationalises "the same policy narrative" across texts

This is the question asked, and the honest answer is that **NPF does not identify narratives; it identifies narrators, and indexes narratives to them.**

The meso-level model (2018 chapter, Fig. 5.1, p. 188) is explicit: *Policy Actors A have Policy Preference A* → they construct *a* policy narrative from some combination of elements, strategies and beliefs; *Policy Actors B have Policy Preference B* → a competing one. The unit of identity is **one coalition's advocacy for one policy preference**. Two texts belong to the same policy narrative when they come from actors on the same side of the same policy contest and assign the same characters to the same roles.

In coded practice this means: documents are the sampling unit; coders mark presence/absence of hero/villain/victim by named entity, plot type, and moral; results are then **aggregated by coalition**, and the "narrative" is the resulting distribution. Krippendorff's α ≥ .80 is the conventional target, .67–.80 permitting tentative conclusions. Worked example: Blair, B. D., & McCormack, L. (2016), *Research & Politics* 3(1) — hydraulic fracturing in two Colorado newspapers; the victim is the public in both, the villain is usually oil and gas in both, but the **hero differs by outlet** (industry in the conservative paper, environmental organisations in the liberal one), and that difference is what the study reports as two narratives.

Note the implication and the limitation. NPF's identity criterion needs a **known contest with named sides**. Narrative Radar often does not have one: a YouTube narrative frequently has a villain and no organised advocate. We inherit the character-role part of NPF's criterion and have to supply our own substitute for the coalition index.

**Caveat on NPF's empirical strength**, carried over from `01-narrative-psychology.md`: character effects are real but thin. Jones 2014 found hero effects on climate risk perception (~1,500 subjects); Zanocco, Song & Jones 2018 found **no direct effect** on fracking attitudes, only an indirect path through feelings toward the villain. Treat NPF as a good coding ontology with weak causal backing, not as a demonstrated theory of persuasion.

> **The identity criterion this field would use.** Two texts carry the same narrative when they share the same configuration of Entman's four functions — same problem, same diagnosed cause, same moral judgment, same remedy — and, in NPF terms, assign the same characters to the same roles in service of the same policy preference. Surface wording, evidence and examples are free to vary. A change in the **remedy/moral**, or in **who is cast as villain**, is a different frame. A change in what the text refuses to consider is also a different frame.

## 2. Social representations and collective belief

Moscovici, S. (1973). Foreword to C. Herzlich, *Health and Illness: A Social Psychological Analysis*, pp. ix–xiv. The standard definition, universally quoted from this foreword: a social representation is a system of values, ideas and practices with a twofold function — to establish an order that lets people orientate themselves in their material and social world, and to enable communication among members of a community.

The two processes by which a new idea becomes a shared belief:

- **Anchoring** — the unfamiliar is classified and named by reference to something already known, which imports the existing thing's evaluations along with its category. A new virus becomes "a kind of flu," and inherits flu's threat level.
- **Objectification** — the abstraction is turned into something concrete and picturable: a metaphor, a diagram, a person, an image. Once objectified, the representation is treated as a fact of the world rather than a construction.

Moscovici, S. (1988). Notes Towards a Description of Social Representations. *European Journal of Social Psychology* 18(3), 211–250, adds a three-way typology that is directly useful to a tracker:

- **hegemonic** — shared across a whole large group, uncontested, usually invisible;
- **emancipated** — a subgroup's own autonomous version, circulating alongside others;
- **polemical** — generated by conflict, defined by what it opposes.

Most of what Narrative Radar tracks is **polemical**. That matters: a polemical representation's identity is partly constituted by its antagonist. Change the antagonist and you have changed the representation, even if the propositional content survives.

### The structural approach — where the operational test lives

Abric's central core theory (*noyau central*) is the part of this literature with a real identity criterion. The representation has a dual structure: a **central core**, socially determined, stable, which gives every other element its meaning (Wolter 2018 quotes its two functions as **generative** — other elements "acquire meaning and specific value" through it — and **organisational** — it "determines the nature of the relationships"); and a **peripheral system**, individual and context-dependent, which "acts as a bumper," absorbing contradiction and letting the group adapt without changing the core.

The standard reading in this tradition — two representations are different when at least one element of their central cores differs — is cited widely enough to be the field's working rule, but I could not open a primary Abric text to verify the exact wording, so treat the formulation as the field's consensus rather than a verified quotation.

What **is** verified, and is better anyway, is the **method**: *mise en cause* (challenging the core). Wolter, R. (2018), *Psico-USF* 23(4), 621–631, describes it as a removal test — if most people agree that without element X it is no longer the object, X is central; if they accept that the object can exist without X, X is peripheral. Method paper: Guimelli, C. (1993). Locating the central core of social representations: Towards a method. *EJSP* 23(5), 555–559.

This is the single most directly portable technique in the whole brief. It converts an abstract identity question into a one-question empirical test, and it is the ancestor of the removal test in §The Test.

> **The identity criterion this field would use.** Take the elements of each version and ask, of each one, "remove this and is it still the same story to the people telling it?" The elements that survive that question are the central core. Two versions are the same representation when their cores match; they are variants when they differ only in the periphery — which is exactly where contradictions, local details and defensive add-ons are supposed to accumulate. Additionally: if the representation is polemical, its antagonist is part of its core.

## 3. Schema, scripts and mental models

### Bartlett — reconstruction, and what actually replicated

Bartlett, F. C. (1932). *Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology*. Cambridge University Press. Two ideas: **effort after meaning** (people assimilate the unfamiliar to the familiar in order to make it make sense) and **schema**, defined around pp. 200–201 as an active organisation of past reactions or past experiences — Bartlett stressed it was active, developing and operating *en masse*, and later cognitive psychology's static "schema" is a departure from him.

**Replication status — flag this, it is usually reported wrongly in both directions.** Bergman, E. T., & Roediger, H. L. (1999). Can Bartlett's repeated reproduction experiments be replicated? *Memory & Cognition* 27(6), 937–947. Read in full. Earlier partial attempts had failed; this one succeeded. Subjects heard "The War of the Ghosts," recalled at 15 min, 1 week and (n = 13) 6 months.

- Accurate propositions recalled fell from **.26 at 15 min to .12 at one week** (strict instructions).
- The **proportion of errors** rose from .57 to .75 across the week; the proportion of **major** distortions rose from .15 to .20.
- Distorted material was **not** merely better retained — an item-level analysis found correct items forgotten at .26 and distorted items at .24, so the distortion is produced by reconstruction, not by selective survival.
- Subjects **imported entirely new propositions** at long delays.

So Bartlett's core claim holds: the gist is kept and reworked, the detail is lost, and new detail is invented to fill the shape. Recall instructions ("be exact") helped only on the first test and made no difference a week later.

### Schank & Abelson — scripts

Schank, R. C., & Abelson, R. P. (1977). *Scripts, Plans, Goals and Understanding*. Erlbaum. A script is a stereotyped sequence of actions defining a well-known situation, with **props**, **roles**, **entry conditions**, **scenes** and **results** — the restaurant script (entering, ordering, eating, exiting; customer, waiter, cook, cashier, owner) being the canonical case. The point for us is that a script is a *slot structure*: the roles are the invariant, the fillers are free.

### Bower, Black & Turner 1979 — the best single experiment in this brief

Bower, G. H., Black, J. B., & Turner, T. J. (1979). Scripts in Memory for Text. *Cognitive Psychology* 11(2), 177–220. Read in full. Subjects read one, two or three different stories instantiating the same underlying script (e.g. the "visiting a health professional" script), then took a recognition test.

- They intruded and falsely recognised **script actions that were never stated**, and this rose with the number of same-script stories read: mean ratings for unstated actions rose 3.91 → 4.62 → 4.81 for one, two, three instances.
- For inferences not stated in **any** text, ratings rose **3.49 → 4.75** with additional instances.
- The decisive result is the third confusion type. Subjects confused sentences whose only similarity was that they **fill the same abstract role in the script**: "The nurse checked John's blood pressure and weight" against "The dental hygienist checked and x-rayed Bill's teeth." Ratings rose **2.73 → 4.73**. Bower et al. call this remarkable, because the two sentences share no meaning at the surface at all — only the role.

This is the empirical foundation for the whole enterprise. **People themselves merge stories at the role level, and surface dissimilarity does not stop them.** Two videos with no shared vocabulary, no shared named entities, and no shared evidence can be the same thing to the audience if the role structure matches.

Bower et al. note the open problem, which is also ours: is a text about a cardiologist an instance of the cardiologist script, the doctor script, or the health-professional script? Script identity has no natural granularity; it has to be chosen.

### Gist versus detail, and survival of contradiction

- **Fuzzy-trace theory** (Brainerd, C. J., & Reyna, V. F., 2002, *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 11(5), 164–169, and subsequent work): two parallel traces, **verbatim** (surface detail) and **gist** (meaning). Verbatim decays fast; gist persists and remains usable. False memories arise from gist-consistency. This is the mechanism behind "details mutate, gist is retained."
- **Continued influence.** Walter, N., & Tukachinsky, R. (2020), *Communication Research* 47(2), 155–177 — meta-analysis, 32 studies, N = 6,527: correction does not eliminate the effect of misinformation (r = −.05, p = .045). Corrections work worse when the claim was repeated before correction, came from a credible source, or arrived late. Ecker, U. K. H., et al. (2022). The psychological drivers of misinformation belief and its resistance to correction. *Nature Reviews Psychology* 1, 13–29: a bare retraction leaves a **causal gap** in the mental model, and a correction that supplies a plausible alternative cause works far better than one that just negates.

That gives the mechanism by which a story survives contradiction: the audience is holding a causal model, not a proposition list. Refuting a proposition leaves the model standing and the model reaches for a replacement part. **Which is exactly what a mutation is.**

> **The identity criterion this field would use.** Same narrative = same causal gist and the same role structure. The people who hold the story do not distinguish variants that fill the same roles, even when the surface has nothing in common (Bower et al.). Verbatim content is expected to drift and should never be part of the identity test. A mechanism swapped in after a refutation is the normal repair of a surviving model, not a new model.

## 4. Myth and semiotics

### Barthes — the concept has unlimited signifiers

Barthes, R. (1957/1972). "Myth Today," in *Mythologies*. Myth is not a kind of object but a mode of signification: "myth is a type of speech," "a system of communication… a message," and it "is not defined by the object of its message, but by the way in which it utters this message." It is a **second-order** semiological system: a complete sign from the first order becomes the empty form of the second.

The passage that answers our question directly:

> "A signified can have several signifiers… It has at its disposal an unlimited mass of signifiers: I can find a thousand Latin sentences… a thousand images which signify to me French imperiality."

And the method that follows from it:

> "This repetition of the concept through different forms is precious to the mythologist, it allows him to decipher the myth: it is the insistence of a kind of behaviour which reveals its intention."

That is a working detector. **The myth is the concept; the forms are unlimited and disposable; you identify the myth by the insistence of a repeated intention across visibly different surfaces.** Barthes adds the two claims for which he is best known — myth's task is "giving an historical intention a natural justification, and making contingency appear eternal," and "myth is depoliticized speech." Both describe what the mature narrative is doing once it stops being argued for: it stops presenting itself as a claim and starts presenting itself as background.

Barthes has no stopping rule. Nothing in "Myth Today" tells you when two concepts are one concept. That is the field's weakness and it is inherited by everything downstream of it.

### Lévi-Strauss — the myth is all its versions

Lévi-Strauss, C. (1955). The Structural Study of Myth. *Journal of American Folklore* 68(270), 428–444; reprinted in *Structural Anthropology*, from which the page numbers below are taken.

- "Its substance does not lie in its style, its original music, or its syntax, but in the **story** which it tells" (174), and "the mythical value of the myth remains preserved, even through the worst translation" (174). Myth is the one form of speech where **translation loss does not destroy identity** — which is precisely the property we need.
- The unit is not the sentence but the **gross constituent unit** (later, **mytheme**): "the true constituent units of a myth are not the isolated relations but *bundles of such relations*" (175–176). Identity is at the level of a relation between terms, not at the level of a term.
- And the crux: all versions of a myth are the myth. "All versions of any myth are equally true as long as we continue to feel they represent the myth" (181).

That last clause is doing real work and is almost always dropped when the line is quoted. Lévi-Strauss's identity criterion has a **felt** component: the corpus is bounded by the community's own sense that these are the same story. He does not offer a structural test that runs without it.

### Propp — the function is constant, the character is not

Propp, V. (1928/1968). *Morphology of the Folktale*. Propp's finding across 100 Russian tales: the **functions** of the dramatis personae are the stable, constant elements, independent of who fulfils them and how; there are 31 of them, and their sequence is constrained. Characters and their attributes are the variables.

**This directly contradicts NPF and the computational work in §7**, which make the character-to-role assignment the identity of the narrative. Both cannot be right at the same level of analysis, and the reconciliation is that they are not at the same level: Propp is classifying at the tale-type tier (very coarse — hundreds of tales collapse into one morphology), NPF at the political-contest tier (fine — a different villain is a different fight). For a tracker, NPF's tier is the useful one and Propp's is too coarse to be actionable. Say so rather than blending them.

The folklorists' own century-old working taxonomy is the **Aarne–Thompson–Uther tale type index**, which assigns a number (e.g. ATU 333, Little Red Riding Hood) to a plot, and treats all recorded tellings as variants of it. It is a practical, imperfect, argued-over system — which is what Narrative Radar's catalogue will also be.

### Empirical test of "same story, different surface"

Tehrani, J. J. (2013). The Phylogeny of Little Red Riding Hood. *PLoS ONE* 8(11), e78871. Phylogenetic methods applied to 58 variants coded on 72 plot traits. Result: ATU 333 and ATU 123 ("The Wolf and the Kids") share an ancient common root but are now genuinely distinct types, and the African and East Asian tales previously lumped with ATU 333 cluster separately. This is the best demonstration that "is this the same story?" can be answered with evidence rather than assertion — by coding discrete plot traits and letting the tree fall out, rather than by eyeballing resemblance.

### Contested: the monomyth

**Flag this clearly. The hero's journey is not usable as scholarship.** Campbell's monomyth (*The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, 1949) is rejected by academic folklorists on method, not taste:

- Alan Dundes, "Folkloristics in the Twenty-First Century" (AFS Invited Presidential Plenary Address, 2004), *Journal of American Folklore* 118(470), 385–408: Dundes's much-quoted judgement is that no single idea promulgated by amateurs has done more harm to serious folklore study than the notion of archetype. (I verified the article's existence and pagination; the quotation is reproduced consistently in secondary literature, and I did not open the original page.)
- The standing methodological charge, made by Dundes, Toelken and others, is **source-selection bias**: the pattern was built by citing tales that fit and omitting ones that do not (Oedipus Rex, Rumpelstiltskin, most creation myths).

The practical danger for us is specific and severe. The monomyth is an **unfalsifiable merge rule**: at a high enough level of abstraction any two stories share a structure, so a Campbell-style criterion collapses the whole catalogue into one narrative. Any identity test we adopt must have a floor that stops this. See the abstraction guard in §The Test.

> **The identity criterion this field would use.** The story is the concept, not the telling. Two surfaces are the same myth when the same relation between the same terms keeps recurring through visibly different material — recognised by insistence and repetition, not by resemblance (Barthes). All versions belong to the myth, and the corpus is bounded by whether the community still feels them to be the same story (Lévi-Strauss). Propp would go further and say even the characters may change, but that tier is too coarse for a tracker and we do not adopt it.

## 5. Conspiracy belief as a system

### Goertzel 1994 — what he actually claimed

Goertzel, T. (1994). Belief in Conspiracy Theories. *Political Psychology* 15(4), 731–742. Read in full (author's typescript). Telephone survey, April 1992, **348 randomly selected residents** of three southwestern New Jersey counties, stratified to over-represent Camden; ten conspiracy items.

Findings: the nine-item correlation matrix showed moderate-to-high correlations "including several that have no strong logical or topical connection"; the first principal factor explained **37.8%** of common variance; the summated scale had α = .78. Belief correlated with anomia (r = .43), low interpersonal trust (r = −.37), job insecurity (r = .21), minority status (r = .44), and weakly negatively with age (r = −.21).

The monological claim is in the discussion, borrowed from Benjamin Goertzel's *Chaotic Logic*, and is a **conjecture, not a result**:

> "In a monological belief system, each of the beliefs serves as evidence for each of the other beliefs."

Goertzel says so himself: to test it "we would need time series data to determine how change in belief about one conspiracy effects change in belief in another," and he proposes content analysis or depth interviews instead. **The famous claim was never tested in the paper that is credited with it.** He also explicitly notes that the flying-saucer item behaved differently from the rest — uncorrelated with anomia — and speculated it taps a different belief system. That is a boundary finding hiding inside the founding paper.

### Did it replicate?

The **correlation** replicates robustly and everywhere. The **monological interpretation** does not survive contact with the details.

- **Sutton, R. M., & Douglas, K. M. (2014).** Examining the monological nature of conspiracy theories. In *Power, Politics, and Paranoia*, CUP. Argue that not all conspiracy theories are mutually supportive, that there is no evidence conspiracy theorists display the claimed closed epistemology, and that the positive correlations have plausible alternative explanations (shared personality or attitudinal causes rather than mutual support).
- **Franks, B., Bangerter, A., Bauer, M. W., Hall, M., & Noort, M. C. (2017).** Beyond "Monologicality"? Exploring Conspiracist Worldviews. *Frontiers in Psychology* 8, 861. Interview study, 21 participants — small, qualitative, South East England. Propose a **spectrum of five types** of believer rather than one system, and conclude that monologicality "may be less a defining feature of believing in CTs, but rather a variable consequence of the set of CTs believed." Their alternative is shared *symbolic resources* rather than a single logical system.
- **Wood, M. J., & Gray, D. (2019).** Right-wing authoritarianism as a predictor of pro-establishment versus anti-establishment conspiracy theories. *Personality and Individual Differences* 138, 163–166. Two MTurk samples (N = 294, 200). RWA correlated strongly with **pro-establishment** conspiracy theories and weakly with **anti-establishment** ones; the gap is explained by attitudes toward the alleged perpetrator. **Conspiracy beliefs are not one undifferentiated bloc: who the villain is predicts who believes it.**

### The contradictory-beliefs finding, and its live dispute

- **Wood, M. J., Douglas, K. M., & Sutton, R. M. (2012).** Dead and Alive: Beliefs in Contradictory Conspiracy Theories. *Social Psychological and Personality Science* 3(6), 767–773. Endorsement of mutually incompatible theories was positively correlated (Diana murdered / Diana faked it; bin Laden already dead / still alive), mediated by belief that authorities are covering something up.
- **van Prooijen, J.-W., Wahring, I., Mausolf, L., Mulas, N., & Shwan, S. (2023).** Just Dead, Not Alive: Reconsidering Belief in Contradictory Conspiracy Theories. *Psychological Science* 34(6). Four preregistered studies, **N = 7,641**, 28 contradictory pairs. The positive correlation replicated every time — but was **driven mainly by respondents who believed the official account** (disbelieving both also produces a positive correlation). Among those who rejected the official story, the correlation was inconsistent.
- **Miani, A., Cruz, N., & Lewandowsky, S. (2026).** Still very much dead and alive: Incoherence in conspiracism as a departure from Bayesian rationality. *PNAS Nexus*. Reanalysis of eight studies, N = 8,590, modelling incoherence as departure from Bayesian coherence rather than as raw correlation; strongest conspiracy believers had a ~91% probability of being incoherent.

**Status: unresolved and actively contested.** Do not build on "conspiracy theorists believe contradictory things" as a settled fact. What is safe to say is weaker and still useful: incoherence between variants does not stop people treating them as the same concern.

### Conspiracy mentality — a disposition, not a narrative

- **Bruder, M., Haffke, P., Neave, N., Nouripanah, N., & Imhoff, R. (2013).** Measuring individual differences in generic beliefs in conspiracy theories across cultures: Conspiracy Mentality Questionnaire. *Frontiers in Psychology* 4, 225.
- **Imhoff, R., & Bruder, M. (2014).** Speaking (Un-)Truth to Power: Conspiracy Mentality as a Generalised Political Attitude. *European Journal of Personality* 28(1), 25–43. Conspiracy mentality is a stable generalised political attitude, distinct from RWA and SDO.
- **Brotherton, R., French, C. C., & Pickering, A. D. (2013).** Measuring belief in conspiracy theories: the Generic Conspiracist Beliefs Scale. *Frontiers in Psychology* 4, 279. The GCB resolves into **five related but distinct factors** — Government Malfeasance, Malevolent Global Conspiracies, Extraterrestrial Cover-up, Personal Wellbeing, Control of Information — explaining 60.9% of variance.

The GCB factor structure is itself an argument against monologicality at the level we care about: the *disposition* is general, but the *content* partitions into separable families.

### The most portable operationalisation in this literature

Samory, M., & Mitra, T. (2018). "The Government Spies Using Our Webcams": The Language of Conspiracy Theories in Online Discussions. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction* 2 (CSCW), art. 152. Over 200,000 users and ~6 million comments from r/conspiracy across ten years. They extract conspiracy statements with a syntactic rule as an **agent–action–target triple** and cluster those into 33 topics.

That is a real, published, scaled identity criterion for exactly our object: **who is doing what to whom**. It is the direct ancestor of the test proposed below.

> **The identity criterion this field would use.** One theory = one agent–action–target triple (Samory & Mitra). A *network* of theories = triples that share an agent or a cover-up premise but differ in action or target. The general disposition (conspiracy mentality) is a property of people, not of narratives, and must never be used as a merge rule — if it were, every conspiracy video would be one narrative, which Wood & Gray's pro- versus anti-establishment split falsifies. Goertzel's monological claim is a conjecture he himself said was untested; cite it as history, not as grounds.

## 6. Rumour and meme transmission

### Allport & Postman 1947 — the three processes, and the correction

Allport, G. W., & Postman, L. (1947). *The Psychology of Rumor*. Holt. Serial reproduction, ~thirty-odd experiments, chains of six or seven people; the first person describes a projected slide while looking at it, each subsequent person hears only the previous account. Three processes:

- **Levelling** — the account gets shorter, sharper and more easily told; details drop out, fast at first then asymptotically.
- **Sharpening** — a small number of surviving details are given disproportionate prominence.
- **Assimilation** — the remainder is reorganised to fit the teller's habits, interests and expectations.

They also proposed the "basic law of rumour," R ≈ i × a (intensity as a product of importance and ambiguity). Treat that as a heuristic; it was never established quantitatively.

**Contested — and the most-cited example in this literature is the one most often misreported.** Treadway, M., & McCloskey, M. (1987). Cite unseen: Distortions of the Allport and Postman rumor study in the eyewitness testimony literature. *Law and Human Behavior* 11(1), 19–25. Read in full.

- What Allport & Postman actually reported: with the subway picture (a **white** man holding a razor confronting a Black man), "in over half of the experiments… at some stage in the series of reports the black man… was said to hold the razor" (1947, p. 111). That is *at some stage in the chain*, over half of *chains*.
- What the eyewitness-testimony literature turned it into: that ~50% of *observers who saw the picture* misremembered the razor as being in the Black man's hand (Buckhout 1974; Yarmey 1979; and onward into legal scholarship and expert testimony).
- Treadway & McCloskey's correction: **only the first person in the chain ever saw the picture**, and described it while looking at it. The razor shift was made by people later in the chain who had only heard a description. It is a transmission effect, not a perception effect.
- And in a footnote they undercut the stereotype interpretation itself: there was **no control condition** with the razor initially in the Black man's hand, and their own pilot work found that many objects in the scene (a hat, a newspaper) "migrate" along the chain. The shift may be plain memory failure.

There is a useful irony here, and it is not decoration: the canonical rumour finding was itself **levelled, sharpened and assimilated** by the scientific literature that transmitted it. Assume the same is happening to anything we read second-hand about a narrative.

**Also contested:** the serial-reproduction paradigm's ecological validity. Field studies of real rumour (Caplow 1947 on Army rumour; and the review in DiFonzo, N., & Bordia, P., 2007, *Rumor Psychology*, APA) find that in settings where a group has a stake in accuracy, rumours can become *more* accurate over time. Levelling and sharpening are laboratory regularities, not laws of the field.

### Shin et al. — reworded resurfacing

Shin, J., Jian, L., Driscoll, K., & Bar, F. (2018). The diffusion of misinformation on social media: Temporal pattern, message, and source. *Computers in Human Behavior* 83, 278–287. (Companion: Shin et al. 2017, *New Media & Society* 19(8), 1214–1235.) Life-cycle tracing of **17 popular political rumours** on Twitter over **13 months** of the 2012 US presidential election.

- **False rumours come back repeatedly after initial publication; true rumours do not.** That asymmetry is the headline result and it is the best evidence available that recurrence is a property of the false ones.
- **Resurgence is accompanied by textual change, generally toward exaggeration** — a more intense, more extreme version each time. The trend stopped abruptly after the election.
- **How they come back:** partisan sites repackage the old rumour as news and influential accounts reintroduce it.

Note the methodological point hiding in that design. Shin et al. had to decide, by hand, that a reworded 2012 resurgence was the *same* rumour as the 2011 original. Their unit of identity was the rumour's **subject and allegation**, held constant while wording escalated. That is the same choice we face, made by researchers, at the scale of 17 items.

### Memetics and its critics

- **Dawkins, R. (1976).** *The Selfish Gene*, ch. 11 — the meme as a replicator. **Blackmore, S. (1999).** *The Meme Machine* — the strong programme.
- **The central critique: cultural transmission is reconstruction, not replication.** Sperber, D. (1996). *Explaining Culture: A Naturalistic Approach*. Blackwell — the "epidemiology of representations." Receivers do not copy; they infer and rebuild, guided by domain-specific cognitive dispositions. Because those dispositions are shared, reconstructions converge on **cultural attractors** — positions in the space of possible variants toward which transformations tend. Similarity across a population is therefore explained by convergence, not by fidelity. See also Atran, S. (2001). The trouble with memes. *Human Nature* 12(4), 351–381; and Claidière, N., Scott-Phillips, T. C., & Sperber, D. (2014). How Darwinian is cultural evolution? *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 369, 20130368.
- **Empirical test of attraction:** Lerique, S., & Roth, C. (2018). The Semantic Drift of Quotations in Blogspace: A Case Study in Short-Term Cultural Evolution. *Cognitive Science* 42(1), 188–219. Tracking word substitutions in quotations as they propagate across blogs: words that are harder to recall are the ones substituted, and easier-to-recall words are produced in their place. The variables examined showed a **single attractor** and were generally **contractile** — variants move toward a centre rather than dispersing.

The consequence for identity is important and cuts against naive versioning: **variation is not random drift away from an original, it is directed motion toward an attractor.** So the "canonical" version of a narrative is not the first one we saw; it is the one variants are converging on. A tracker that anchors identity to the earliest observed telling will mislabel the mature form as a deviation.

> **The identity criterion this field would use.** Same rumour = same subject and same allegation, with wording, magnitude and evidentiary trappings free to escalate (Shin et al.). Same meme = same attractor: variants that keep converging on one formulation are one thing, however different their surfaces (Sperber; Lerique & Roth). And the operational tell that spreaders treat a variant as the same story is **resurgence** — the reappearance of a dormant claim, repackaged as news, with the allegation intact and the intensity raised.

## 7. Working taxonomies in practice

### Claim matching — and the lesson that abstract criteria fail

Kazemi, A., Garimella, K., Shahi, G. K., Gaffney, D., & Hale, S. A. (2021). Claim Matching Beyond English to Scale Global Fact-Checking. *ACL-IJCNLP 2021*, 4504–4517. Read in full. Meedan; WhatsApp tiplines and public groups plus fact-check headlines in Bengali, English, Hindi, Malayalam, Tamil.

Their definition of the task is the field's practical identity criterion:

> "identifying pairs of textual messages containing claims that can be served with one fact-check"

**But the crucial finding is about what happens when you hand that criterion to annotators.** They report plainly that asking annotators "to speculate about whether some hypothetical fact-check could cover both statements was unhelpful." So they replaced it with a concrete four-point similarity judgement — Very Similar / Somewhat Similar / Somewhat Dissimilar / Very Dissimilar — and built the codebook so that **"Very Similar" pairs are by construction the pairs one fact-check could serve.**

Results: Randolph's marginal-free κ of **0.64–0.91** by language (bn .64–.68, en .69, hi .90, ml .91, ta .85); only **6–21%** of candidate pairs were rated Very Similar. And notably, **claim similarity was an easier task than claim detection** — agreement on "are these the same claim?" was consistently higher than on "is this a claim at all?"

Two directly actionable lessons for Narrative Radar. First: **do not ask a human or a model the abstract question.** Ask a concrete one engineered to be equivalent. Second: the hard part is not matching, it is deciding what the unit is in the first place — so spend the definitional effort there.

Related: Shaar, S., Babulkov, N., Da San Martino, G., & Nakov, P. (2020). That is a Known Lie: Detecting Previously Fact-Checked Claims. *ACL 2020*, 3607–3618 — formalises retrieval of a matching previously-verified claim, motivated explicitly by the fact that viral claims come back after a while and politicians repeat themselves. The identity criterion is the same one: does an existing fact-check already cover this?

Full Fact, "Towards a common definition of claim matching" (Oct 2021) is worth noting as the practitioners' own statement that the field lacks a shared definition — this is unsettled inside fact-checking too.

### What the big misinformation datasets actually do

Ng, L. H. X., & Carley, K. M. (2021). The Coronavirus is a Bioweapon: Analysing Coronavirus Fact-Checked Stories. arXiv:2104.01215. **6,139** fact-checked stories from Poynter's CoronaVirusFacts/DatosCoronaVirus Alliance database. Method: three annotators labelled roughly a third of the corpus into **14 manually derived story types** (Case Occurrences, Commercial Activity/Promotion, Conspiracy, Correction/Calling Out, Emergency Responses, Fake Cures, Fake/True Fact or Prevention, Fake/True Public Health Responses, Public Figures, …), disagreements resolved by majority vote; K-means on BERT embeddings separately produced 6 clusters; a BERT classifier reached 59% accuracy on stories and 43% on tweets (~4.5× and ~2.7× baseline).

**Be honest about what this shows.** The CoronaVirusFacts alliance itself is a **database of individual fact-checks**, not a narrative catalogue. Its structure is: one row per fact-check, with country, language, rating and the checking organisation. Researchers who want topics **impose them afterwards**, by hand, on a per-project basis. There is no published, stable, cross-project identity criterion for "one narrative" in the fact-checking ecosystem — and the categories that do exist are at the **topic** tier, not the narrative tier. That is a gap, not a resource. Nobody is going to hand us this.

The structured metadata that *is* standardised — schema.org's **ClaimReview**, with its `claimReviewed` field — is likewise claim-tier: one proposition, one verdict.

### Computational narrative clustering — the closest thing to our problem

- **Ash, E., Gauthier, G., & Widmer, P. (2024).** Relatio: Text Semantics Capture Political and Economic Narratives. *Political Analysis* 32(1), 115–132. Semantic role labelling extracts **agent–verb–patient** statements from raw text; entities and verbs are clustered into coherent groups; narratives are the recurring relations between those groups. Applied to the US Congressional Record. Open-source (`relatio`). The identity criterion is explicit and mechanical: **same agent group, same verb group, same patient group**.
- **Das, R., Deshmukh, A., Leto, A., Naaman, Z., Lee, I.-T., & Pacheco, M. L. (2026).** A Structured Clustering Approach for Inducing Media Narratives. arXiv:2604.10368. Read in full. Builds **causal narrative chains** (pairs of `(verb, object)` events linked by a causal relation), annotates the characters and their **narrative roles**, then clusters the chains *using character and role information as clustering constraints*.

Das et al.'s evaluation metric is the single most directly usable definition of narrative identity I found anywhere in this brief. Their **exact-match purity** treats as identical the narrative chains sharing "the complete canonical character-role configuration," where a configuration is a full mapping of character groups to roles — their own worked example being `Immigrants: Victim, Law Enforcement: Threat, Politicians: Hero`. Their looser **role-based purity** decomposes the chain and scores individual character-role pairs, which gives a graded partial-match score — exactly what a "mutation" verdict needs.

Reported numbers: Krippendorff's α of **0.76** on causal-relation annotation (0.71 immigration, 0.81 gun control, 200 event pairs per dataset, third annotator breaking ties); their causal extractor reached F1 58.46 (±2.06), retaining ~85% of the teacher model; constrained clustering beat unconstrained on all metrics. Framing categories come from the Media Frames Corpus (Card et al. 2015), 15 categories — again, topic tier.

Also relevant for feasibility: Stammbach, D., Antoniak, M., & Ash, E. (2022). Heroes, Villains, and Victims, and GPT-3: Automated Extraction of Character Roles Without Training Data. *WNU @ NAACL* — role extraction works zero-shot.

> **The identity criterion this field would use.** In practice, and with no exceptions I could find: **a structured tuple, not a similarity score.** Fact-checkers: one claim = what one fact-check can serve, operationalised as a concrete similarity judgement because the abstract one is unusable. Computational narrative work: one narrative = one canonical mapping of character groups to narrative roles over the same causal chain. Embedding similarity is used only to *generate candidates*; the identity decision is always made on structure.

## Where the fields conflict

State these rather than blending them.

1. **Propp versus NPF and the computational work — is the villain part of the identity?** Propp: no, the actor is the free variable and the function is the constant. NPF and Das et al.: yes, the character-to-role mapping *is* the narrative. They are at different tiers. For a tracker of live political claims, the NPF tier is the useful one; Propp's would collapse "the Fed is destroying the dollar" and "BRICS is destroying the dollar" into one item, which is plainly wrong for our users. **We adopt NPF and reject Propp at this tier, explicitly.**

2. **Fact-checkers versus framing scholars — claim or frame?** A fact-check's unit is a checkable proposition; a frame's unit is a configuration of problem/cause/judgment/remedy. One narrative contains many claims and survives the refutation of most of them (Walter & Tukachinsky 2020). **A claim-tier criterion will shatter our narratives into fragments; a topic-tier criterion will fuse them into mush.** Neither field is offering us our tier; we have to define it and say so.

3. **Semiotics versus everyone on the stopping rule.** Barthes and Lévi-Strauss give an excellent account of how a story survives surface change and a poor account of when two stories are two. Lévi-Strauss's honest answer is the community's own felt sense; Campbell's answer is what happens when you remove that constraint. **Any structural criterion needs a floor.**

4. **Monologicality — the correlation is real, the system is not.** Goertzel's data support a general disposition; his monological claim was a conjecture he acknowledged was untested; Wood & Gray show the content partitions by target. And the contradictory-beliefs literature is currently unresolved (van Prooijen 2023 versus Miani et al. 2026). **Do not merge narratives on the grounds that the same audience believes both.**

5. **Levelling/sharpening — a lab regularity, not a law.** Allport & Postman's canonical result is widely misreported (Treadway & McCloskey 1987), and field studies find rumours can become more accurate where accuracy matters. Shin et al.'s escalation finding is better evidence for our setting, because it comes from the field and from the right medium.

## The test

One test, drawn from the strongest evidence, applicable by a person or a model in under a minute.

Its lineage: NPF's minimum bar (at least one character plus a referent) and Stone's causal-mechanism types; Samory & Mitra's agent–action–target triple; Das et al.'s canonical character-role configuration; Abric's *mise en cause* removal test; and Kazemi et al.'s hard-won lesson that the abstract question must be replaced by a concrete one.

### Step 1 — Write the core sentence for each side. Do not compare first.

Force each set of videos into exactly this shape, one sentence, no hedging:

> **[AGENT]** is doing **[MECHANISM]** to **[TARGET]**, and the result will be **[OUTCOME, with its clock]**.

Four slots. If you cannot fill AGENT and OUTCOME, it is not a narrative yet — it is a topic or an event (consistent with `SYSTEM.md` §1 and with NPF's minimum bar).

Write both sentences **before** looking at them side by side. This is the Kazemi discipline: annotators could not answer "would one fact-check serve both?" but could answer a concrete structured question built to mean the same thing.

### Step 2 — Compare slot by slot, in this fixed order.

| What differs | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Nothing; only wording, named individuals, figures, dates, clips, evidence | **Same narrative, same season** |
| **OUTCOME** differs | **Two narratives** — stop here, no further checks |
| **AGENT** differs, or the mechanism changes blame from accident to intent (or back) | **Two narratives** |
| **MECHANISM** differs, but AGENT, TARGET and OUTCOME hold | **Mutation** — one narrative, new season |
| **TARGET** differs but agent and outcome hold | **Mutation**, unless the change in target changes who the villain is — then two |

Order matters: outcome is checked first because it is the identity-bearing slot (Entman's remedy; NPF's moral), and agent second because NPF and Das et al. both make the character-role mapping constitutive.

The intent clause is the one refinement the literature forces on the existing `SYSTEM.md` rule. NPF codes causal mechanisms with Stone's four types — **intentional, inadvertent, accidental, mechanical** — and a shift between them changes the moral, which changes the frame. "The dollar is collapsing because the Fed is incompetent" and "the dollar is collapsing because BRICS is deliberately killing it" share an outcome, but they are not one narrative with two mechanisms; they are two, because blame moved and the villain changed.

### Step 3 — Two confirmations, ten seconds each.

- **Removal test** (Abric's *mise en cause*). Take the slot that differs. Ask: *if you took this out, would the people telling it still say it is the same story?* If yes, it is peripheral — same narrative. If no, it is core — two narratives. This is the field-validated way to distinguish core from periphery and it is the tiebreaker when Step 2 is ambiguous.
- **Shared-correction test** (Kazemi; Shaar et al.). Ask: *would one refutation land on both?* If a single correction would be felt by holders of both as attacking their story, they are one. If refuting one leaves the other completely untouched, they are two.

### Step 4 — The abstraction guard. Apply always.

If you can only make the two the same by climbing to a higher abstraction — "elites are hiding something," "the system is rigged," "the hero overcomes the trial" — **stop and record two narratives.** You have left the narrative tier for the theme tier, and that is the failure mode that made the monomyth unusable as scholarship (Dundes 2005; the source-selection charge). The floor: **the outcome slot must stay specific enough to be wrong.** If the merged sentence's outcome could not, in principle, fail to happen, the merge is invalid.

### Step 5 — Record, do not discard.

When the verdict is **mutation**, log both the retained slots and the changed one, with dates. Two reasons from the evidence:

- Ecker et al. 2022 — a mechanism swap after a refutation is the normal repair of a surviving causal model, so mutations mark the moments a narrative was attacked and survived. That is the most informative event in a narrative's life.
- Lerique & Roth 2018 — variants converge on an attractor, so the mature form is not a deviation from the first version we happened to see. Keep the version history and let the canonical form be identified as the one variants are converging on, not the earliest.

And when two narratives share three of four slots, record the shared parent. Entman's Iraq observation — homogeneous framing at one level, competing frames at another — means a parent relation is often the truth, and flattening it loses the structure that makes the catalogue navigable.

## What this changes in the current system

`SYSTEM.md` §1 currently says: a change of **mechanism** with the same outcome is a mutation; a change of **outcome** is a new narrative. That rule is well founded — outcome corresponds to Entman's remedy and NPF's moral, the slots both fields treat as identity-bearing — and nothing here overturns it.

Three refinements the evidence supports:

1. **Add the agent slot.** The rule is currently silent on the villain. NPF's coded practice and Das et al.'s exact-match purity both make the character-to-role mapping constitutive of narrative identity, and Wood & Gray show that *who the villain is* predicts who believes it. A mechanism change that also changes who is to blame is not a mutation.
2. **Add the intent clause.** Stone's causal typology as used in NPF: a shift between accidental, inadvertent, mechanical and intentional causation changes the moral, and therefore the narrative — even when agent and outcome are unchanged.
3. **Do not anchor identity to the first version observed.** Cultural attraction (Sperber; Lerique & Roth 2018) says variants move toward a centre. The canonical statement of a narrative should be the convergent form, re-derived as evidence accumulates, not the earliest telling.

One thing the evidence says we should **not** do: merge on audience overlap. The same people believing two things is a fact about the disposition (conspiracy mentality), not about the narratives, and the monological claim that would license the merge is a 1994 conjecture its own author said was untested.
