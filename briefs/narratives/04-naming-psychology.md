# Research — What a name does to a claim

Researcher brief, 2026-09-19. Scope: what psychology establishes about giving a claim a **name or label** before the reader sees evidence.
Companion to `01-narrative-psychology.md` (transportation, illusory truth, framing, out-group effects are covered there and are not repeated).

**Verification.** Every source below was checked against the publisher record, an open-access PDF, or the indexed abstract (OpenAlex / Semantic Scholar). Where I read the full text I give in-text statistics. Where I could only verify the record and the abstract, I say so. Nothing here is cited from memory.
Abbreviations: MA = meta-analysis · d / r / η² / φ = effect sizes · pp = percentage points.

---

## Three findings that change the premise

**1. A pejorative category label barely moves belief — and is mostly a *symptom* of disbelief.**
Two independent experimental programmes (Wood 2016; Douglas, van Prooijen & Sutton 2022) find no detectable effect of calling something a "conspiracy theory" on how much people believe it. What *does* move is the reverse arrow: people reach for the label when they already disbelieve. The label reports the namer's stance, it does not install one in the reader.

**2. The backfire effect has been substantially walked back — by its own originators.**
Worldview backfire and familiarity backfire are both non-robust. The real failure mode is not "correction makes belief stronger"; it is **decay** (Nyhan 2021) and **collateral scepticism** (Clayton et al. 2020; Hoes et al. 2024) — warnings and general "be sceptical" framings drag down belief in *true* material too. Design against the second problem, not the first.

**3. "Concrete wording feels truer" failed replication.**
Hansen & Wänke (2010, *PSPB* 36(11):1576–88) found concretely worded statements were judged more likely true. Henderson, Vallée-Tourangeau & Simons (2019, *Collabra: Psychology* 5(1):19) ran a preregistered dual-site replication (n = 253 in person, n = 237 online) and found dz = 0.08 [−0.03, 0.18] and dz = 0.11 [−0.01, 0.22] — both consistent with zero. So "make the name concrete and it will feel truer" is **not** an evidence-backed rule. Concreteness may still help comprehension and recall; it does not buy credibility.

## 1. Labelling and category effects

### The "conspiracy theory" label — robust null, twice

- **Wood 2016** (*Political Psychology* 37(5):695–705; two MTurk experiments).
  - Exp 1 (N = 150): historical and speculative theories judged no less likely when labelled "conspiracy theories" than when labelled "ideas."
  - Exp 2 (N = 802): a news article about fictitious corruption allegations was endorsed no less when the allegations were labelled "conspiracy theories."
  - No moderation by attention to the stimulus, and no moderation by the participant's own negative view of conspiracy theories.
- **Douglas, van Prooijen & Sutton 2022** (*British Journal of Psychology* 113(3):575–590; four studies, preregistration and OSF data posted). Full text read.
  - Study 1 (N = 170) replicated and extended Wood with seven belief measures. Only one of seven reached significance (p = .042, η² = .02) and **that effect vanished** once the 43 participants who failed the manipulation check were dropped. All other p > .10, all other η² < .02.
  - A striking manipulation-check result: only 1 participant in the "conspiracy theory" condition mislabelled the statements, versus 42 in the "idea" condition (χ²(1, N = 170) = 52.33, p < .001, Cramér's V = .56). **Readers supply the label themselves whether or not you print it.**
  - Studies 2–4 (Ns = 199, 150, 151) reverse the arrow. Study 3 (within-subjects): implausible statements were more likely to be called conspiracy theories, F(1,147) = 18.84, η² = .11, and their believers more likely to be called conspiracy theorists, F(1,147) = 17.57, η² = .11. Study 4 (between-subjects, ruling out demand): η² = .16 and η² = .24 respectively.
  - Authors' conclusion: the label is **a consequence, not a cause**, of (dis)belief.

**Contested.** Dentith, Husting & Orr 2023 (*Society* 61(2):189–196) argue it is premature to conclude the label has no rhetorical power, on conceptual grounds plus a similar quasi-experimental design. It is a single paper, argumentative rather than a clean high-powered experiment, and it does not overturn the two nulls above. Flagged, not weighted.

### The stigma is real, but it attaches to the belief, not to your label

- **Lantian, Muller, Nurra, Klein, Berjot & Pantazi 2018** (*EJSP* 48(7):939–954). Inducing French participants to write in support of a Charlie Hebdo conspiracy theory raised their anticipated negative evaluation and fear of social exclusion. (A 2025 replication exists — *Collabra: Psychology* 11(1), "Conspiracy Theories as a Source of Social Stigma in the Age of Corona"; I verified the record, not its effect sizes.)
- Read together with Douglas 2022: people already feel the stigma of the belief. Adding a pejorative name does not increase the deterrent; it mostly announces which side the writer is on.

### Sociological labelling theory: theory, not effect sizes

Becker (*Outsiders*, 1963) and Lemert (1951) hold that deviance is produced by social reaction rather than by the act. Husting & Orr 2007 (*Symbolic Interaction*) extend this to "conspiracy theorist" as a device of exclusion. **These are theoretical and qualitative.** There is no quantitative labelling-theory literature that predicts how a reader's belief shifts when a claim is given a category name. Do not cite effect sizes here; there are none.

### Noun labels essentialise

- **Gelman & Heyman 1999** (*Psychological Science* 10(6):489–493; N = 115 children, ages 5 and 7). "She is a carrot-eater" made the trait judged more stable across time and context than "She eats carrots whenever she can." A noun label turns a behaviour into a kind of person.
- Caveat: developmental sample. The adult generalisation is plausible but I found no preregistered adult replication of the noun/verb asymmetry. Treat as suggestive.

### Names as frames: real, large, and historically unstable

- **Schuldt, Konrath & Schwarz 2011** (*POQ* 75(1):115–124; N = 2,267, RAND American Life Panel). Republicans endorsed the phenomenon at 44.0% under "global warming" versus 60.2% under "climate change"; Democrats were unmoved (86.9% vs 86.4%). Partisan gap fell from 42.9 pp to 26.2 pp.
- **But it did not hold up.** Soutter & Mõttus 2020 (*J. Environmental Psychology* 69:101413; US, UK, Australia) found no wording effect. Schuldt, Enns, Konrath & Schwarz 2020 (same journal) ran a 2018 US replication (N = 1,017): overall χ²(1) = 1.90, p = .17; the Republican-specific effect χ²(1, N = 255) = 0.82, p = .37, φ = 0.06. The original authors caution it is premature to say the terms never matter, but their own data show the effect gone.
- **Read this as the central lesson of the naming literature: a name's power is contingent on the political meaning already attached to the words, and that meaning moves.** A name that is a lever this year may be inert next year.

### "Fake news" as a label is captured, not neutral

- **van der Linden, Panagopoulos & Roozenbeek 2020** (*Media, Culture & Society* 42(3):460–470; national US sample, N = 1,000). 75% of conservatives associate CNN with "fake news"; 59% of liberals say the same of Fox. The label is a partisan weapon rather than an epistemic category.
- **Michael & Breaux 2021** (*Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* 6) reach the same conclusion: what counts as "fake news" tracks political affiliation.

**What this means for how we name a narrative.** A pejorative category word ("conspiracy," "hoax," "fake") is a near-zero lever on belief, a strong signal of our own stance, and liable to be read as partisan — so the name should describe the *claim*, not sentence it.

## 2. Warning and tagging research

### Tag wording: definite beats hedged, and the size is modest

- **Clayton, Blair, Busam, Forstner, … Nyhan 2020** (*Political Behavior* 42(4):1073–1095; preregistered, N = 2,994). Full text read.

  | Treatment | Mean accuracy of false headlines (4-pt scale) | Effect | Cohen's d |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | No warning, no tag (baseline) | 1.96 | — | — |
  | General warning only | 1.90 | −0.08 (pooled) | 0.08 |
  | "Disputed" tag | 1.73 | −0.24 | 0.26 |
  | "Rated false" tag | 1.62 | −0.34 | 0.38 |

  - In binary terms: acceptance of a false headline fell from 29% to 19% with "Disputed" (−10 pp, 95% CI −7 to −13) and to 16% with "Rated false" (−13 pp, 95% CI −11 to −17).
  - **"Rated false" beat "Disputed" by −0.11 (p < .01).** The authors' reading: "Disputed" implies mixed evidence or legitimate disagreement; "Rated false" states a verdict.
  - Effects did **not** vary by whether the headline was politically congenial.
  - No significant implied-truth spillover from the *tags* here (point estimates 0.02 for "Disputed," 0.04 for "Rated false"; CIs cross zero) — but see below.
  - **The general warning had a cost:** it reduced perceived accuracy of *true* headlines by −0.12 (p < .01, d = 0.12) — a larger hit than the −0.08 it inflicted on false ones.

### The implied truth effect

- **Pennycook, Bear, Collins & Rand 2020** (*Management Science* 66(11):4944–4957).
  - Study 1 (n = 5,271 MTurk): warnings modestly reduced perceived accuracy of tagged false headlines, **and** raised perceived accuracy of *untagged* false headlines relative to control. The absence of a tag was read as validation.
  - Study 2 (n = 1,568): same pattern for sharing intentions.
  - **The fix is in the paper:** adding verification labels to some *true* headlines eliminated, and slightly reversed, the implied truth effect. Ambiguity about "unchecked vs. checked-and-clean" is what does the damage.
  - Status: the core implied-truth result is robust in these two studies; Clayton et al. did not reproduce a significant tag-spillover, so treat the magnitude as uncertain while treating the mechanism as real and cheap to defuse.

### Warning labels overall — this is one of the better-replicated interventions

- **Martel & Rand 2024** (*Nature Human Behaviour*, "Fact-checker warning labels are effective even for those who distrust fact-checkers"; internal MA of 21 experiments, total N = 14,133). Warning labels cut belief in false headlines by **27.6%** and sharing by **24.7%**. Among participants who strongly distrust fact-checkers the effects shrink but survive: **12.9%** belief, **16.7%** sharing.
- **Martel & Rand 2023** (*Current Opinion in Psychology*) reviews the same literature and concludes labels are broadly effective across party lines, with size depending on implementation.

### Accuracy prompts: real but small, and the original estimate was inflated

- **Pennycook, McPhetres, Zhang, Lu & Rand 2020** (*Psychological Science*; two studies, >1,700 US adults). A single accuracy reminder "nearly tripled" truth discernment in sharing intentions.
- **Roozenbeek, Freeman & van der Linden 2021** (*Psychological Science* 32(7):1169–1178; preregistered direct replication, SCORE programme). **Stage 1 (n = 701) failed outright, p = .67.** After a second round (pooled N = 1,583) they found a small significant interaction (uncorrected p = .017; treatment d = 0.14, control d = 0.10) — far below the original.
- **Pennycook & Rand 2022** (*Nature Communications* 13:2333; internal MA of 20 experiments, N = 26,863). Accuracy prompts improve sharing discernment mainly by cutting sharing of false headlines ~10% relative to control. Not moderated by gender, race, ideology, education; larger for older, more reflective, more attentive participants. Also **Pennycook et al. 2021** (*Nature* 592:590–595) for the Twitter field experiment.
- Honest summary: the effect exists and generalises, but it is roughly a 10% shift, not a tripling.

### The two documented backfires of warning systems

- **Freeze, Baumgartner, Bruno, et al. 2021** (*Political Behavior* 43(4):1433–1465) — the **tainted truth effect**: warnings wrongly applied to *accurate* news reduce its credibility and impair political memory. (Record and abstract verified; I did not extract sample sizes.)
- **Hoes, Aitken, Zhang, Gackowski & Wojcieszak 2024** (*Nature Human Behaviour* 8:1545–1553; three countries — US n = 2,008, Poland n = 2,147, Hong Kong n = 1,972; total n = 6,127 across 21 experiments). Fact-checking, media-literacy tips and media coverage of misinformation all reduced belief in false information **and all reduced the credibility of true information.** Generalised scepticism, not calibrated scepticism.

**What this means for how we name a narrative.** Use verdict-definite wording over hedged wording, label the *true/checked* material too (not only the dubious), and never let the product read as a global "don't trust anything" warning — that is the one framing measured to damage true content more than false.

## 3. Name as retrieval cue

### A name given before the evidence controls what the evidence becomes

- **Bransford & Johnson 1972** (*Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 11(6):717–726). A title supplied **before** an ambiguous passage sharply raised comprehension ratings and recall; the same title supplied **after** did not. This is the closest direct analogue to what Narrative Radar does — a name shown before evidence. Caveat: 1972, small samples, no modern preregistered replication located; the finding is textbook-canonical but has not been through the replication machinery.
- **Ecker, Lewandowsky, Chang & Pillai 2014** (*J. Experimental Psychology: Applied* 20(4):323–335). Headlines that emphasise secondary content — without being false — distorted memory, inferential reasoning, behavioural intentions, and even impressions of a face pictured alongside. The mechanism the authors give is exactly ours: the headline **constrains subsequent processing** and readers fail to update away from the first interpretation.

### Gist vs. verbatim

- **Reyna 2021** (*PNAS* 118(15), e1912441117). Fuzzy-trace theory: verbatim and gist traces are encoded in parallel; verbatim decays fast, gist persists and drives decisions. People show a *fuzzy-processing preference* — they reason with the simplest gist that will do. Reyna's applied claim is that communication should target the correct gist rather than piling on verbatim facts.
- **Brainerd & Reyna** (e.g. *Advances in Child Development and Behavior* 28:41–100, 2002) for the dual-trace machinery.
- This is a theory-plus-review base, not an effect size. Its practical implication for us is specific: **the name is the gist.** Whatever the reader carries away a week later is closer to our five words than to our evidence block.

### The familiarity backfire effect: claimed, then substantially retracted

Chronology of the walk-back, all verified:

- **Swire, Ecker & Lewandowsky 2017** (*JEP: Learning, Memory & Cognition* 43(12)). Myth-repeating retractions worked; belief regressed over a week; adults over 65 sustained the correction least well. This is **regression toward baseline, not backfire.**
- **Ecker, Lewandowsky & Chadwick 2020** (*Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* 5:41; three experiments, total N = 1,718). Tested the strongest remaining case — corrections that introduce *novel* misinformation to a fresh audience. **No familiarity backfire.**
- **Swire-Thompson, DeGutis & Lazer 2020** (*JARMAC* 9(3):286–299). Review: backfire effects are "not a robust empirical phenomenon"; much of the apparent evidence is a measurement and design artefact.
- **Prike, Blackley, Swire-Thompson & Ecker 2023** (*Cognitive Research* 8:39; three experiments, total N = 1,156). Standalone corrections did not backfire immediately or after a week. **One caveat survives:** Experiment 3 gave mixed evidence of backfire when participants were sceptical of the correction itself.
- **Ecker, Sharkey & Swire-Thompson 2023** (*PLOS ONE* 18(4):e0281140). Direct attempt to replicate Pluviano et al.'s vaccine myths-vs-facts backfire: **failed to replicate** either familiarity- or fear-driven backfire.

**Current status:** familiarity backfire is not established and should not drive design. What *is* established and remains a genuine risk is plain illusory truth from repeated exposure — a different effect, covered in brief 01.

### The Debunking Handbook 2020 consensus

Lewandowsky, Cook, Ecker et al., *The Debunking Handbook 2020* (consensus document, University of Bristol / Skeptical Science; process and author-selection published in "Under the Hood of The Debunking Handbook 2020"). Full text read. Its structural recommendations:

1. **FACT** — lead with the fact if it is clear, pithy and sticky; simple, concrete, plausible, and it must fit the story. If the facts are too nuanced to summarise, lead instead with why the myth is false.
2. **WARN ABOUT THE MYTH** — forewarn that a myth is coming; mention it once only.
3. **EXPLAIN FALLACY** — say *how* the myth misleads, juxtaposed unmissably with the myth, including why it was believed and why the alternative is right.
4. **FACT** — close by restating the fact, ideally more than once, providing a causal alternative that fills the gap the myth was filling.

Two further lines bear directly on Narrative Radar:
- On prominence, the handbook says corrections should be as prominent as the misinformation — and specifically **"in the headlines, not buried in questions."**
- On unknown myths: if a claim is not spreading, the less said the better. Debunk when it has traction.
- On backfire: it states plainly that recent evidence gives no reason to avoid debunking for fear of backfire.

**What this means for how we name a narrative.** The name is the gist the reader keeps, so it should carry our finding, not the claimant's phrasing — and repeating the claim once, right next to the verdict, is safe.

## 4. Question headlines and uncertainty

### Betteridge's law

Folklore. I found **no peer-reviewed empirical test** of the proposition that question headlines are answered "no." Blog-level counts exist; they are not evidence. State it as folklore or not at all.

### What is actually established: a question plants the proposition

- **Wegner, Wenzlaff, Kerker & Beattie 1981** (*JPSP* 40(5):822–832). Three experiments. A question headline ("Is Karen Downing Associated with Fraudulent Charity?") damaged impressions of the target substantially — the incrimination-through-innuendo effect. Denials also damaged.
- **Letourneau & Gawronski 2024** (*Social Psychology* 55(1):51–61; two preregistered studies, total N = 506). Full text read. Close replication of Wegner's Study 1, plus a new partisanship test.

  | Contrast (vs. neutral control) | Exp 1, N = 150 | Exp 2, N = 356 |
  |---|---|---|
  | Assertion headline | d = 0.72 | d = 0.66 |
  | **Question headline** | **d = 0.50** | **d = 0.43** |
  | Denial headline | d = 0.12 (n.s.) | d = 0.16 |

  - A question headline does roughly **two-thirds to three-quarters of the damage of a flat assertion.**
  - The novel hypotheses failed: effects did **not** differ by whether the target was a political in-group or out-group (Exp 1 interaction F(6,894) = 1.06, p = .384, ηp² = .007; Exp 2 F(6,2130) = 0.89, p = .499). Innuendo is not a partisan-only weapon.
  - The authors' framing is exactly the JAQ problem: asking a question spreads the proposition **without ever making a claim that could be fact-checked.**
- **Clifford & Sullivan 2023** (*Journal of Experimental Political Science*, doi 10.1017/xps.2023.1; preregistered within-subjects experiment inside a panel survey). Exposure to a standard conspiracy *question* significantly raised endorsement of that conspiracy **a week later**. Critically: a question format that **offered an alternative, non-conspiratorial explanation** produced no such effect. (Abstract verified; I could not extract N or effect size from an open copy — flagged.)

### Questions cost credibility and do not reliably buy engagement

- **Janét, Richards & Landrum 2020** (*Journalism Practice* 16(1):35–55). Three headline formats — traditional, forward-referencing, question. Format did **not** affect story selection or engagement, but **question headlines were rated least credible**, for both the headline and the story. (Abstract and record verified; N not extracted.)
- **Scacco & Muddiman 2020** (*New Media & Society* 22(3):429–448). Lab experiment plus a field test across nine local newspaper sites. **Summary headlines beat curiosity/question headlines** on perceived information adequacy, expected clarity, anticipated engagement — and on actual selection behaviour in the field.
- **Lai & Farbrot 2014** (*Social Influence* 9(4):289–299). The pro-question outlier: two field experiments where question headlines out-clicked declaratives, with self-referencing questions ("Is this your new iPhone4?") strongest. But the stimuli were classified-style ads, the outcome was clicks only, and the authors note the effect **varies by topic**. Popular summaries quote very large percentage gains from this paper; those come from small field tests with low base rates and should not be carried into a news product.
- **Gligorić, Lifchits, West & Anderson 2023** (*PLOS ONE* 18(3):e0281682; registered report; 32,487 Upworthy A/B experiments, 24,333 pairs in the confirmatory analysis). The largest headline dataset in existence — and it **did not test question form at all.** It did confirm negative-emotion words (β = 0.18) and, notably, that headline success is hard to predict (54.4% accuracy vs 50% baseline). So the biggest dataset simply does not answer this question.
- **Kuiken, Schuth, Spitters & Marx 2017** (*Digital Journalism*). Observational Blendle data; "contains a question" is one of the tested features and most (not all) clickbait features raised clicks. I could not extract the question-specific coefficient from the available PDF — do not cite a number from this paper.

**What this means for how we name a narrative.** Never put the claim in the name as a question: it implants the proposition at ~70% of the strength of asserting it, it is rated the least credible of the standard formats, and its click advantage is thin and context-dependent.

## 5. Numbers in a label

### Numbers and precision raise perceived credibility

- **Koetsenruijter 2011** (*Newspaper Research Journal* 32(2):74–82). News stories containing numbers were rated more credible than the same stories with quasi-quantifiers ("some," "a few"). Single study, small journal, 2011 — directionally useful, not load-bearing.
- **Jerez-Fernandez, Angulo & Oppenheimer 2014** (*Psychological Science* 25(2):633–635). Precision is read as a cue to the speaker's confidence. Two small studies.
- **Xie & Kronrod 2012** (*Journal of Advertising* 41(4):103–117). Four experiments: numerical precision signals company competence — **moderated by scepticism**, working mainly on low-scepticism audiences. That moderator matters for us: our readers are selected for scepticism.
- Schindler & Yalch 2006 (*Advances in Consumer Research* 33:586–590) is the usual source for "sharp numbers read as research-derived, round numbers as estimates." Conference proceedings, not peer-reviewed at journal standard. Flagged as weak.

### Precision has a ceiling, and a memory problem

- **Loschelder, Friese, Schaerer & Galinsky 2016** (*Psychological Science*; five experiments, 1,320 experts and amateurs across real-estate, jewellery, car and HR negotiations). **The too-much-precision effect:** precision helps linearly for amateurs but shows an inverted U for experts, who read excessive precision as *incompetence*. Mediated by competence attributions.
- **Appelman 2023** (*Electronic News*, doi 10.1177/19312431231190846; N = 300). The most useful finding for us: readers largely **failed to recall or recognise** the numbers and the maths errors in the articles. But readers who *believed* they had seen numbers rated content quality higher, and readers who *believed* they had seen errors rated content and source quality lower — **regardless of experimental condition.** The impression of rigour does the work; the number itself is not retained.
- **Stubenvoll & Matthes 2021** (*Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly*, doi 10.1177/10776990211021800; N = 413). A wrong number anchors judgement **even after a retraction, and even when the reader distrusts the number.** A figure printed in a name is very hard to take back.

### Where the evidence does not exist

I found **no experiment** testing a precise statistic embedded in a short *name or label* (as opposed to a full headline or an advertising claim) on later belief or recall. The literature above is headlines, ads, negotiations and advice-taking. Any number-in-the-name rule is an extrapolation and should be treated as a hypothesis to A/B test, not a finding.

**What this means for how we name a narrative.** One round, defensible, clearly-sourced figure is worth including for the rigour signal — but never a figure we might have to retract, and never precision beyond what we actually measured.

## 6. Naming and identity threat

### Identity-protective cognition is contested, not settled

- The source claim: **Kahan, Peters, Dawson & Slovic 2017** (*Behavioural Public Policy* 1(1):54–86), motivated numeracy — more numerate partisans polarise *more* on identity-relevant data.
- **Persson, Andersson, Koppel, Västfjäll & Tinghög 2021** (*Cognition* 214:104768). Large preregistered replication with a pre-specified analysis plan: **did not find good evidence for motivated numeracy.** Ideologically congruent responding was present but not moderated by numeracy in the predicted way.
- Cross-national work ("Truth over identity? Cultural cognition weakly replicates across 23 countries," *J. Environmental Psychology* 2022) reports weak replication of cultural cognition.
- Conclusion: identity-protective cognition is a real-sounding mechanism with **weak and contested empirical support.** Do not build a naming policy on it as if it were settled.

### Backfire from challenging a belief: rare

- **Nyhan & Reifler 2010** (*Political Behavior* 32(2):303–330) — the original worldview backfire finding.
- **Wood & Porter 2019** (*Political Behavior* 41(1):135–163). Five experiments, >10,100 subjects, 52 issues chosen to be maximally favourable to backfire. **No corrections triggered backfire.**
- **Guess & Coppock 2020** (*BJPS* 50(4):1497–1515). Three experiments, two MTurk and one nationally representative. **No backlash**, even under theoretically favourable conditions. (A corrigendum corrected a coding error affecting reported SDs in Table 1; the conclusion stands.)
- **Nyhan 2021** (*PNAS* 118(15), e1912440117). The original author's own reassessment: the backfire effect was over-generalised by later research and media; corrections generally increase accuracy, and the real problem is that the gain **decays** or is drowned out by elite cues.

### What *does* reliably cost you: controlling language

- **Li & Shi 2026** (*Human Communication Research* 52(1):38–52; MA of 33 independent samples from 28 articles, 146 effect sizes, 2005–2024).

  | Relationship | r | k |
  |---|---|---|
  | High vs. low freedom-threatening language → anger | .21 | 28 |
  | → negative cognitions (counterarguing) | .17 | 25 |
  | → overall reactance | .20 | 53 |
  | Anger → persuasion | −.23 | 42 |
  | Negative cognitions → persuasion | −.18 | 39 |
  | Gain vs. loss framing → reactance | −.02 (n.s.) | 12 |

  So: **wording that tells people what to think reliably produces anger and counterarguing, and those reliably reduce persuasion.** Gain/loss framing does nothing. This, not "backfire," is the measured cost of a bossy or contemptuous name.
- **Rains 2013** (*Human Communication Research* 39(1):47–73; MA k = 20, N = 4,942) established the intertwined anger-plus-counterarguing model that the 2026 MA builds on.

### The ridicule result cuts the other way — and is thin

- **Orosz, Krekó, Paskuj, Tóth-Király, Bőthe & Roland-Lévy 2016** (*Frontiers in Psychology* 7:1525; N = 813, representative Hungarian online sample). Three strategies: rational counter-arguments, **ridiculing those who hold the belief**, and empathising with the targets. Rational arguments **and ridicule both reduced** conspiracy belief; empathy did nothing. Perceived intelligence and competence of the messenger predicted success.
- Single study, one country, 2016, no replication located. It contradicts the naive reactance prediction. **Report it, don't act on it.** A product that ridicules its readers has a reputational and retention cost the study never measured.

### Expert consensus, where evidence runs out

The *Debunking Handbook 2020* advises inclusive language and explicitly warns against stigmatising groups for holding inaccurate beliefs, on the reasoning that stigma polarises rather than updates. That is a consensus judgement of the field's experts, **not a measured effect size.** Label it as such.

### Does neutral attributed naming ("the X story") avoid the problem?

**There is no direct evidence.** I found no experiment comparing a neutral attributed name against a pejorative name for the same claim, measuring identity threat or belief. Say this plainly rather than implying support. The four nearest analogues, each an inference rather than a test:

1. **Clifford & Sullivan 2023.** Offering an *alternative explanation* alongside the claim removed the belief-spreading effect of merely raising the claim. Suggests that framing which names a competing account is safer than framing that only names the claim.
2. **Gelman & Heyman 1999.** Noun labels essentialise the labelled person. Suggests naming the *story* ("the X story") is safer than naming the *believer* ("X truthers"). Developmental data; adult inference untested.
3. **Li & Shi 2026.** The measured lever is controlling/derisive language, not the category label. A descriptive name carries no freedom threat; a contemptuous one does.
4. **Self-affirmation meta-analyses** (Sweeney & Moyer 2015; Epton et al. 2015): d ≈ 0.26–0.32 for reducing defensive processing. A different intervention entirely — it does not test naming and does not transfer to a headline.

**What this means for how we name a narrative.** Name the claim and never the believer, keep the language descriptive rather than directive — and treat "neutral naming protects the reader's identity" as our own untested hypothesis, worth an A/B test, not a cited fact.

## Ranked naming rules the evidence supports

1. **Put our finding in the name, not the claimant's phrasing.** Gist is what survives (Reyna 2021); a title read before the evidence determines what the evidence means (Bransford & Johnson 1972); headlines that lead with secondary content distort memory and reasoning downstream (Ecker et al. 2014). *Strength: strong theory + one strong applied experiment + one canonical but unreplicated classic.*
2. **Never phrase the claim as a question.** A question implants the proposition at d ≈ 0.43–0.50 versus d ≈ 0.66–0.72 for a flat assertion (Letourneau & Gawronski 2024, preregistered, N = 506), raises endorsement a week later (Clifford & Sullivan 2023), and is rated the least credible headline format (Janét et al. 2020; Scacco & Muddiman 2020). *Strength: strong — preregistered replication of a 1981 classic.*
3. **Use verdict-definite wording, not hedged wording.** "Rated false" beat "Disputed" by −0.11 scale points (Clayton et al. 2020, preregistered, N = 2,994); the whole labels literature (Martel & Rand 2024 MA, 21 experiments, N = 14,133) works at 27.6% belief reduction. *Strength: strong.*
4. **Label the checked-and-clean material too, not only the dubious.** The implied truth effect (Pennycook et al. 2020, n = 5,271 + 1,568) was eliminated and slightly reversed by adding verification labels to true items. *Strength: good — one strong paper with the fix built in; magnitude uncertain because Clayton et al. did not reproduce the spillover.*
5. **Name the claim, never the believer.** Noun labels essentialise (Gelman & Heyman 1999); expressing the belief already carries anticipated social exclusion (Lantian et al. 2018); the expert consensus document warns against stigmatising groups. *Strength: moderate — converging indirect evidence, no direct test.*
6. **Keep the language descriptive, never directive or contemptuous.** Freedom-threatening language → anger r = .21, counterarguing r = .17, and both cut persuasion (r = −.23 and −.18) — MA of 33 samples (Li & Shi 2026). *Strength: strong for language style; note the one contrary ridicule study.*
7. **Repeat the claim once, adjacent to the verdict — and only when the claim already has traction.** Familiarity backfire is not established across five studies and reviews (Ecker et al. 2020, N = 1,718; Prike et al. 2023, N = 1,156; Swire-Thompson et al. 2020; Ecker et al. 2023); the handbook recommends exactly one repetition next to the correction, and silence on claims nobody is spreading. *Strength: strong for the null; the handbook structure itself is consensus, not a trial.*
8. **Include at most one round, defensible, sourced figure — and only one we will never retract.** Numbers raise credibility (Koetsenruijter 2011) and precision signals confidence and competence (Jerez-Fernandez et al. 2014; Xie & Kronrod 2012), but the impression of numbers does more work than the numbers themselves (Appelman 2023, N = 300), over-precision reads as incompetence to expert audiences (Loschelder et al. 2016, N = 1,320), and a wrong number anchors judgement even after retraction (Stubenvoll & Matthes 2021, N = 413). *Strength: moderate; and no study has tested a number inside a short name.*
9. **Give the name a fixed, plain-language meaning and keep it stable.** "Global warming" vs "climate change" was worth 16 pp among Republicans in 2011 and nothing at all by 2018–2020 (Schuldt et al. 2011; Soutter & Mõttus 2020; Schuldt et al. 2020). A name's power rides on the meaning already attached to its words, and that meaning moves. *Strength: strong for the instability claim.*
10. **Where a name must convey uncertainty, name the competing explanation rather than hedging the claim.** Clifford & Sullivan 2023's alternative-explanation format removed the belief-spreading effect that a bare question produced. *Strength: single preregistered study; promising and directly on point.*

## Naming choices the evidence warns against

- **A pejorative category word — "conspiracy theory," "hoax," "fake news" — as the name.** Two independent programmes find no belief effect (Wood 2016, N = 150 + 802; Douglas et al. 2022, Study 1 N = 170), the label is mostly a readout of the namer's disbelief (Douglas et al. 2022, Studies 2–4, η² = .11–.24), and readers supply it themselves anyway (of N = 170, just 1 participant mislabelled in the "conspiracy theory" condition versus 42 in the "idea" condition). Meanwhile "fake news" is a captured partisan term (van der Linden et al. 2020, N = 1,000).
- **A question in the name.** See rule 2. It spreads the claim at roughly 70% of assertion strength with none of the accountability, and it is the format readers trust least.
- **A hedged verdict word.** "Disputed" underperforms "Rated false" (Clayton et al. 2020); hedging invites the reading that reasonable people disagree.
- **Naming the people rather than the claim** ("X truthers," "the X crowd"). Noun labels essentialise; the stigma already attaches to the belief; and derisive framing is the measured driver of anger and counterarguing.
- **Any product-level "be sceptical of everything" framing.** The general warning in Clayton et al. hurt *true* headlines more than false ones (−0.12 vs −0.08), and Hoes et al. 2024 (n = 6,127, three countries) found every prominent intervention reduced belief in accurate information alongside false. Precision in the tag, not blanket suspicion.
- **Tagging only the dubious narratives and leaving everything else unmarked.** The implied truth effect turns absence of a tag into an endorsement.
- **Decorative precision.** Spurious decimals read as incompetence to expert readers (Loschelder et al. 2016) and, if wrong, anchor even after retraction and even for readers who distrust the figure (Stubenvoll & Matthes 2021).
- **Relying on "concreteness makes it feel truer."** The linguistic concreteness–truth effect failed a preregistered dual-site replication (Henderson et al. 2019; dz = 0.08 and 0.11, CIs spanning zero).
- **Designing around "backfire."** Worldview backfire did not survive >10,100 subjects across 52 issues (Wood & Porter 2019) or three further experiments (Guess & Coppock 2020), and the original author has said it does not explain misperception durability (Nyhan 2021). Fear of backfire is not a reason to soften a verdict; reactance to bossy language is a reason to soften the *tone*.
- **Ridiculing believers because one study says it works.** Orosz et al. 2016 (N = 813, Hungary, unreplicated) found ridicule reduced conspiracy belief. It is a single national sample that measured belief change and nothing else — not trust, not retention, not our reputation.

## Where the evidence simply does not exist

Stated plainly so we do not over-claim in the product or in a pitch:

- **No study tests a short public *name* for a recurring claim** — all the relevant work is on headlines, tags, survey questions or ad copy. Every rule above is transferred from an adjacent format.
- **No test of neutral attributed naming** ("the X story") against a pejorative name, for belief or for identity threat. Our central hypothesis is untested.
- **No peer-reviewed test of Betteridge's law.**
- **No test of a precise statistic inside a name** (as opposed to a headline or advertising claim).
- **No preregistered adult replication** of the noun-label essentialism asymmetry (Gelman & Heyman 1999 is a children's sample).
- **No modern replication of Bransford & Johnson 1972**, the single closest analogue to a name-before-evidence design.
- **Quantitative labelling theory does not exist.** Becker and Lemert are theory; Husting & Orr is qualitative. Anyone citing "labelling theory" for an effect size is inventing one.
