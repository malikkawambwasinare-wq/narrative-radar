# Research — What makes a narrative captivating and durable

Researcher brief, 2026-09-15. Citations checked against abstracts, publisher records or full text.
Abbreviations: MA = meta-analysis · obs = observational · r/ρ/d = correlation / corrected correlation / standardised difference.

## Two findings that change the premise

**"When Prophecy Fails" is discredited.** The classic claim is that a group whose prophecy fails recruits harder afterwards.
- Kelly (2025, *J. History of the Behavioral Sciences*) used newly unsealed archives: the group was already recruiting before the prophecy date, the leader recanted afterwards, and the group dissolved.
- The researchers interfered, including faking psychic messages.
- Earlier follow-ups also failed (Hardyck & Braden 1962).
- **What this means:** doom narratives don't survive a clear failure. They survive because the deadline stays vague and keeps sliding.

**The Zeigarnik effect doesn't hold as usually told.** The claim is that people remember unfinished things better.
- A 2025 meta-analysis (Ghibellini & Meier, *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications*) found no memory advantage.
- What remains is a tendency to *resume* interrupted tasks.
- "Open loops make stories stick" is folklore.

## 1. Mechanisms

### Narrative transportation (absorption in a story) — robust, small-to-medium effects

- **Green & Brock 2000** (*JPSP*; lab, 4 experiments). Absorbed readers held more story-consistent beliefs and noticed fewer "false notes". Labelling the story fact or fiction made little difference.
- **van Laer et al. 2014** (*JCR*; MA, 76 articles, 132 effect sizes).
  - Effects of transportation: emotion ρ = .57, attitudes ρ = .44, intentions ρ = .31, beliefs ρ = .26.
  - Transported readers raised fewer critical thoughts: ρ = −.20.
  - What drives transportation: identifiable characters ρ = .20, imaginable plot ρ = .29, verisimilitude ρ = .27.
- **Braddock & Dillard 2016** (*Communication Monographs*; MA). Beliefs r = .17, attitudes r = .19, intentions r = .17, behaviour r = .23 (only k = 5 studies for behaviour).
- **Pennington & Hastie 1992** (*JPSP*; lab). Jurors decide by building a causal story, and evidence presented in story order shifts verdicts.

### Framing and story roles — villain/hero effects moderate, thin replication

- **Entman 1993** (theory). A frame does four things: defines a problem, diagnoses a cause, makes a moral judgment, suggests a remedy.
- **Narrative Policy Framework** (Jones & McBeth 2010; theory). A story has setting, characters (hero, villain, victim), plot and moral.
- **Jones 2014** (online experiment, ~1,500 people). The hero character drove climate risk perception and policy preferences.
- **Zanocco, Song & Jones 2018** (experiment). No direct effect on fracking attitudes. The narrative worked only indirectly, through feelings toward the villain.
- **Lee & Feeley 2016** (MA, 41 studies). The identifiable-victim effect is small (r = .05), largest for a single victim.

### Emotion, negativity and out-group hostility

- **Berger & Milkman 2012** (*JMR*; obs NYT most-emailed list + lab). High-arousal emotion drives sharing.
  - +1 SD anger: 34% higher odds of making the list. +1 SD awe: 30%.
  - Sadness reduces sharing.
- **Robertson et al. 2023** (*Nature Human Behaviour*; ~105,000 Upworthy headline A/B variants).
  - Each negative word raised click-through 2.3%; each positive word lowered it 1.0%.
  - Surprises: anger had no effect, fear *lowered* clicks, sadness raised them.
  - A 2025 robustness report confirmed the negativity boost but not the positivity penalty.
  - A preprint on 6.1M Facebook news posts found negative posts got ~15% fewer likes.
- **Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden 2021** (*PNAS*; obs, 2.7M posts). Each out-group word raised share odds 67%: about 4.8× the effect of negative-affect words and 6.7× moral-emotional words.
- **Brady et al. 2025** (*PNAS Nexus*; preregistered replication N = 849,266 + MA of 27 studies). Each moral-emotional word raises shares 13% (the 2017 original estimated 20%). One of five datasets reversed.
- **Tannenbaum et al. 2015** (*Psychological Bulletin*; MA, 127 studies). Fear appeals work, d = 0.29, more with efficacy statements and one-time asks.

### Why prophecy and doom narratives persist

- **Tetlock 2005** (*Expert Political Judgment*; obs, ~20 years of forecasts). Experts protect failed calls with "off on timing" and "close call" excuses.
- **Walter & Tukachinsky 2020** (MA, 32 studies). Corrections don't fully undo misinformation. They work worse when the claim was repeated, came from a credible source, or the correction came late.
- **Kruglanski & Webster 1996** (theory + lab). Need for closure: people "seize" an answer, then "freeze" on it.
- **Whitson & Galinsky 2008** (*Science*): lack of control increases illusory pattern perception. **Did not replicate.**
  - van Elk & Lodder 2018: 7 studies, Bayesian evidence against the effect.
  - Stojanov & Halberstadt 2020 (MA): d = −0.05, not significant.
- **Douglas, Sutton & Cichocka 2017; Douglas et al. 2019** (reviews). Conspiracy belief serves three motives: epistemic (understanding), existential (safety, control) and social (a positive image of self and group).
- **Bowes, Costello & Tasimi 2023** (*Psychological Bulletin*; MA, 170 studies, N = 158,473, correlational). Correlations with conspiracy belief:

  | Factor | Correlation |
  |---|---|
  | Social threat | .56 |
  | Belief in a dangerous world | .39 |
  | Existential threat | .34 |
  | Collective narcissism | .34 |
  | Low trust | −.26 |
  | Lack of control | −.17 |
  | Need for uniqueness | .16 |
  | Need for closure | .10 |

  **Threat is the core ingredient; need for closure is weak.**

### Hidden knowledge, identity and trusted creators

- **Imhoff & Lamberty 2017** (*EJSP*; correlational + experiment). People high in conspiracy mentality endorsed a fictitious theory more when told only a *minority* believed it.
- **Lantian et al. 2017.** Conspiracy believers feel they hold scarce information. Weak: both experiments marginal (Study 4 p = .081), significant only pooled.
- **Kahan et al. 2017** (identity-protective cognition): more numerate people polarise more. **Contested:** a preregistered replication failed (Persson et al. 2021).
- **Tukachinsky, Walter & Saucier 2020** (MA, k = 120; correlational). Parasocial relationships relate strongly to perceived similarity, transportation and persuasion.

### Curiosity gaps

- **Loewenstein 1994** (theory). Curiosity is the discomfort of a noticed information gap.
- **Aubin Le Quéré & Matias 2025** (*Scientific Reports*; registered report + MA of 8,977 headline experiments). Vagueness has an inverted-U effect on clicks: it helps when competing headlines are too concrete and hurts when they're already vague.
- No evidence that curiosity gaps make a narrative last.

### Repetition and fluency (illusory truth) — among the most robust effects here

- **Dechêne et al. 2010** (MA, 51 studies). Repeated statements feel truer: d = .39–.50.
- **Fazio et al. 2015.** The effect holds even when people know the correct answer.
- **Pennycook, Cannon & Rand 2018.** A single exposure to a fake headline raised perceived accuracy a week later.
- **Hassan & Barber 2021.** Up to 27 repetitions; diminishing returns.
- **Weaver et al. 2007** (*JPSP*). One repetitive voice gets taken as evidence the opinion is widespread: "a repetitive voice can sound like a chorus."

### Rumour spread and persistence

- **Allport & Postman 1947** (theory). Rumour strength ≈ importance × ambiguity.
- **Vosoughi, Roy & Aral 2018** (*Science*; obs, ~126,000 cascades).
  - False news was 70% more likely to be retweeted and reached 1,500 people about 6× faster.
  - It was more novel, and replies to it showed fear, disgust and surprise.
  - Humans spread it, not bots. The novelty link is correlational.
- **Juul & Ugander 2021** (*PNAS*). Structural differences vanish once cascades are size-matched; false news is simply more "infectious". Partly contested.
- **Shin et al. 2018** (*Computers in Human Behavior*; 17 rumours over 13 months). False rumours resurfaced repeatedly in modified form, pushed by partisan sites; true ones didn't. **Best direct evidence on persistence.**

### Emotional arcs

- **Reagan et al. 2016.** Six core emotional arcs; some arcs got more downloads. Weak link to engagement.
- **Toubia, Berger & Eliashberg 2021.** Semantic pace predicts success, but the direction depends on genre. Low priority for us.

## 2. Anatomy of a captivating narrative (ranked by evidence)

| # | Ingredient | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Repetition across voices and time** | Multiple meta-analyses; one exposure is enough; persistence comes from reworded resurfacing |
| 2 | **A causal story with identifiable characters** | Transportation meta-analyses; the juror story model. Absorption cuts counter-arguing |
| 3 | **A named villain or out-group** | Large observational sharing effects (+67% per out-group word); experimental villain effect (Zanocco) |
| 4 | **High-arousal threat** | Anger and anxiety drive sharing; fear appeals d = .29. Negativity's effect on clicks is mixed |
| 5 | **Threat to people like you** | Threat and dangerous-world beliefs are the strongest conspiracy correlates (.34–.56). The identifiable-victim effect alone is small |
| 6 | **A remedy** | Entman's fourth function; fear appeals work better with efficacy. Extrapolated from health messaging |
| 7 | **A clock that can slide** | "Off on timing" keeps forecasts alive; ambiguity fuels rumour. A hard, falsified deadline tends to *end* movements |
| 8 | **A trusted creator** | Parasocial MA, correlational |
| 9 | **Hidden truth / insider status** | Need for uniqueness r = .16; one clean experiment; marginal replications; novelty correlates |
| 10 | **An unresolved question** | Drives clicks on an inverted U. No durability evidence; Zeigarnik unsupported |

## 3. Naming implications

These are inferences from the evidence above, not findings.

- **Name the claim in its own words**, since that's what viewers recognise, together with the structure that holds people (cause, villain, clock, remedy) and its age or track record.
  - Example: "Housing crash: 'next year' since 2012".
  - Example: "Seed oils as hidden cause of disease".
- **Attribute the claim; don't assert it.** Under illusory truth, a narrative's name is itself one more repetition.
  - "Seed Oils Are Poison" as a bare label adds fluency to the claim.
  - "The seed-oil-poison narrative" labels a story instead.
- **A name turns manipulative when it:**
  - adds villain, out-group or moral-emotional words the narrative doesn't use (the strongest measured sharing triggers);
  - uses vague curiosity-gap wording ("What they're hiding about…");
  - casts believers as villains or dupes (invites identity defence; contested);
  - implies insider status, or makes the platform the hero.
- **Honest test:** would a believer recognise the name as a fair statement of what they believe?

## 4. Detection implications

**From titles** (methods validated on headlines and tweets):
- negative and moral-emotional word lists (Robertson; Brady)
- out-group and villain entities (Rathje's dictionaries)
- arousal and threat words
- second-person victim framing ("your savings")
- clock markers ("by 2027", "next year", "before it's too late")
- headline vagueness (Aubin Le Quéré & Matias scale)
- secret-knowledge phrases ("the truth about", "they don't want you to know")
- remedy cues ("how to protect")

**Needs transcripts:**
- Entman's four functions, especially cause, moral judgment and remedy
- character roles and plot coherence
- identifiable characters and vivid imagery
- in-group "we" and direct-address parasocial cues
- how failures get excused ("off on timing", "close call")

**Needs the corpus over time:**
- repetition of the same claim across creators, the core durability signal
- reworded resurfacing (Shin)
- dated predictions matched to outcomes (the "since 2012" track record)
