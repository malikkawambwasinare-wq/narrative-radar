# The psychological order — how Narrative Radar should change what a viewer does next

Synthesis of four research briefs (2026-09-15), all citations checked by the researchers:
[01 YouTube's recommender](01-youtube-recommender.md) · [02 attention capture](02-attention-capture.md) · [03 interventions](03-interventions.md) · [04 sequencing and design](04-sequencing-and-design.md).

Evidence strength: **Strong** = meta-analysis or large replication. **Moderate** = several experiments or a field RCT. **Weak** = one study or theory. **Untested** = nobody has run it.

---

## 1. What we are actually up against

**YouTube published its objective.**
- The 2016 system ranked by expected watch time (Covington et al.). Its own authors warned that ranking by clicks "often promotes deceptive videos".
- The 2019 system blends predicted engagement and satisfaction with hand-tuned weights (Zhao et al.).
- Since 2021 it optimises "valued watchtime", scored from surveys (Goodrow).
- Every version optimises for *the next minute*. None optimises for *the viewer having understood the topic*.

**The popular story is mostly wrong for today's YouTube.** Since 2019, on desktop, for US political content, recommendations are *not* the main road to extreme content (Hosseinmardi 2021, 2024; Chen et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025):
- 1.7% of viewers account for 80% of watch time on alternative channels.
- Those views arrive mostly through **subscriptions** and **links shared outside YouTube** (about half).
- Recommendations *match* what a viewer already watches, and are less extreme than the viewer's own picks.
- The damage is **narrowness**, not escalation.

**The hook is two mechanisms working together.** The founder's own observation — "it reinforces what I think but never gives the conclusion" — matches the research:
- **Agreement is rewarding.** People prefer information that confirms their view (selective exposure; Hart et al. 2009 meta-analysis). The bias is real on both left and right: r = .245 (Ditto et al. 2019). Strong.
- **An open gap pulls.** Curiosity is the felt discomfort of a known gap, strongest when the answer feels close (Loewenstein 1994). People will accept unpleasant outcomes just to close one (Hsee & Ruan 2016). Moderate.
- **Repetition builds belief.** Each recycled video adds belief, with the biggest jump on the second exposure (Dechêne et al. 2010 meta-analysis; Hassan & Barber 2021). Strong.
- **Nothing ever ends.** The feed offers no natural stopping point (Rixen et al. 2023). Autoplay adds about 21 minutes a day (Schaffner et al. 2025). Moderate.

**Put together:** agreement keeps the viewer inside one camp, the gap keeps them chasing inside it, repetition makes it feel confirmed, and there is no finish line. That is the "watch one angle and act on it" loop we exist to break.

## 2. The design principle: same hooks, a closed loop

We don't abolish the hooks. We use them honestly and **close the loop YouTube keeps open**:

| YouTube | Narrative Radar |
|---|---|
| Reinforces the viewer's camp | Meets the viewer where they are — one line, not a repeat |
| Opens a gap the next video never closes | Opens a *concrete, answerable* gap and **closes it on the page** |
| Repetition feels like corroboration | Repetition shown as **one source, repeated N times** |
| The next video, forever | An explicit **"you've seen every side"** ending |
| Optimises minutes | Optimises **understanding per minute**, measured by regret and "was this worth it" |

**The closure is the reward.** YouTube structurally can't offer it, because closing the loop ends the session.

**One hard truth about effect sizes.** Every intervention in the literature is small:
- Lab effects are d ≈ 0.1–0.3.
- Platform effects are low single-digit percentages.
- Information-only nudges come out at d ≈ 0 once missing unpublished studies are accounted for (Maier et al. 2022).
- Field effects run about one-sixth of lab effects (DellaVigna & Linos 2022).

What survives is **context and skill at the moment of decision**, not verdicts or warnings. Our edge is not one clever trick. It is doing several small, well-evidenced things *in the right order*, at the one moment that matters: when someone has a video in hand.

## 3. The psychological order for a narrative page

Steps 1–5 happen before the viewer presses play.

**1. The pattern before the video.**
- *What it says:* "This video is 1 of 33 in *[narrative]*. Its core claim first appeared in 2021 and has been repeated in 14 videos."
- *Why:*
  - Whatever comes first anchors engaged viewers (Haugtvedt & Wegener 1994).
  - Pattern-level framing shifts attention from the single story to the system (Iyengar 1991; conditional).
  - Counts beat percentages (natural frequencies: correct reasoning ~4% → ~24%; strong).
  - Showing repetition as one source counters illusory truth (strong).
- *Never:* autoplay the video, or make its thumbnail or title the hero.
- *Risk:* reads as spin. Keep it purely descriptive.

**2. One question, answered immediately.**
- *What it says:* "When do you think this claim first appeared?" or "Did the last predictions like this come true?" One tap, skippable.
- *Why:*
  - Prequestions boost learning of exactly what they ask about: g ≈ 0.54, but ≈ 0 for anything else (preregistered meta-analyses; strong).
  - A concrete gap closed at once is curiosity used well.
- *Risk:* friction and drop-off. Some low-knowledge learners disengage (ManyClasses 2).

**3. The map.**
- *What it shows:* 3–5 named claims and the camps, with this video highlighted.
- *Why:*
  - Naming the parts before the details improves understanding (pre-training).
  - Cues that show how material is organized help: g = 0.53 for retention (strong).
- *Risk:* overload. Hard cap on items; details on demand.

**4. Weight of evidence, before any camp speaks.**
- *What it says:* "Dated predictions from this camp: 3 of 14 came true", or "11 of 77 videos cite outcome trials".
- *Why:*
  - Presenting sides as equals creates false balance (Dixon & Clarke 2013; Koehler 2016).
  - A weight-of-evidence statement placed first reduces that harm, though it doesn't eliminate it (Imundo & Rapp 2022; moderate).
- *Risks:*
  - Reactance (contested).
  - **An unreliable track record would destroy trust.** Showing a source's record is untested anywhere; we would be first.

**5. Camps sized by evidence, each with its strongest case and its rebuttal.**
- *Why:*
  - Two-sided messages that *refute* the other side beat one-sided ones. Two-sided without refutation is *worse* (O'Keefe 1999; strong).
  - Asking "how strong is the other side's case?" sharply reduced one-sided overconfidence (Brenner et al. 1996; weak, small samples).
- *Never:*
  - Equal columns.
  - Raw exposure to the other camp — people following an opposing account became more entrenched (Bail et al. 2018).
  - Stance badges alone — they increased selective exposure (Gao et al. 2018).

**6. Watching, with context pinned to the moment.**
- *What it shows:* "This claim: first seen 2021 · 2 of 9 similar forecasts held."
- *Why:*
  - Corrections shown *after* exposure improve later discernment most (Brashier et al. 2021).
  - Name the manipulation technique ("false dichotomy at 4:12"). Technique prebunks from videos or text held about 78% of their effect at 29 days; games faded by day 9 (Maertens et al. 2025).
- *Risk:* restating a claim builds familiarity. Summarise once.

**7. The ending.**
- *What it says:* "You've now seen 3 of 3 camps." Offer 1–3 chosen next videos. No autoplay, no endless rail.
- *Why:*
  - Partitions create decision points (Cheema & Soman 2008).
  - Autoplay off cut viewing about 21 minutes a day.
  - Playlists and search support sense of agency; autoplay undermines it (Lukoff et al. 2021).
- *Risk:* fewer minutes. That's the point. **Measure understanding and regret, not minutes.**

**Before acting on a video** (share, save, "act on this"): ask "how do you know this is true?"
- Improves discernment (Fazio 2020; accuracy prompts cut false sharing ~10% in a meta-analysis).
- Small and short-lived, but nearly free.

## 4. Audit of what we have already built

**Keep**

| Built | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Finite Series with numbered episodes | Keep | Stopping points and partitions; no autoplay |
| "Removed by our verdict" fold | Keep | Makes repetition visible; nothing is hidden silently |
| Explanation open first | Keep | Advance organizer / pre-training (small gains) |
| ✓ read / ◌ provisional on *every* video | Keep | Labelling everything avoids the implied truth effect (Pennycook et al. 2020) |
| Title-bait filter | Keep, go further | Naming the technique is a light prebunk. Put the technique name on the card, not only in a filter |
| Hook titles closed on the page | Keep | Headlines perform best at intermediate concreteness (Upworthy, 27,616 tests); the gap closes on our page |
| Prediction ledger | Keep, but test | Untested anywhere. Run our own A/B before claiming it changes trust |

**Fix**

| Built | Problem | Fix | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Camp stage: one equal-sized person per camp** | False balance. 33 vs 7 videos drawn as equals | Show a weight-of-evidence line *above* the stage. Make size or emphasis reflect evidence and record, not video count. Give each placard a rebuttal line | Koehler 2016; Imundo & Rapp 2022; O'Keefe 1999 |
| **Side cards in symmetric columns** | Same false-balance problem | Asymmetric layout; weight of evidence first | Same |
| **Headline percentages** ("14% cite outcome trials") | Percentages are harder to reason with | "11 of 77 videos" | Natural frequencies, strong |
| **Claim status only on some claims** | Implied truth effect | Every claim carries a status: checked / contested / not yet checked | Pennycook et al. 2020 |
| **Paste result** | Leads with a verdict chip | Lead with the pattern (step 1), then the question (step 2) | Order effects; prequestions |

**Reconsider**

| Built | Risk | Fix | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **"Most active narratives" top 5 and momentum charts** | Popularity cues change what people choose and trust. On a viewer's first screen they pull attention toward the hottest (often doom) stories | Keep them in a **creator view**. Or pair every count with evidence status | Salganik et al. 2006; Muchnik et al. 2013; Avram et al. 2020 |
| **Any "search it yourself" pattern** (we have none — keep it that way) | Telling people to search increased belief in false news | Be the curated second source | Aslett et al. 2024, *Nature* |

## 5. What not to do

- Autoplay, or lead with the creator's framing.
- Show equal "Side A vs Side B" columns, or size camps by video count or views.
- Show percentages, trust scores or opaque meters where counts work.
- Label only some claims.
- Use generic "beware misinformation" banners — they lower belief in true content too.
- Make games the main tool — mostly generic scepticism, gone in about 9 days.
- Tell people to go and search.
- Give the other side raw, without its rebuttal.
- Correct people publicly or with "you've been fooled" copy — they then shared lower-quality, more partisan content (Mosleh et al. 2021).
- Rank anything by engagement — negative words gain 2.3% clicks each, out-group words raise share odds 67%. Engagement ranking rebuilds the problem.
- Show consensus meters where no real expert consensus exists. Market and political calls get track records instead.

## 6. Where to show up

About half of problem views arrive **through links shared outside YouTube**. The paste-a-link moment is the right entry point. The next surfaces are wherever links travel:
- the share sheet
- a browser extension that shows the pattern (step 1) on the YouTube watch page itself
- messaging apps

**Defaults beat buttons.** YouTube's "Not interested" cuts similar recommendations only about 11%. The narrative view has to be in the default path, with a one-tap skip. The option to dismiss, not the message, drove the friction effect (Grüning et al. 2023).

## 7. What to measure

Minutes watched is YouTube's metric; it is not ours.

- **North star:** "Was this worth your time?" and regret, both measured.
- **Behaviour:** the share of sessions where a viewer opened another camp before acting; share or save actions that followed "how do you know?"
- **Discernment:** on a small sample, whether viewers tell strong claims from weak ones better *without* the tool afterwards. AI help that did the thinking raised accuracy 21% while in use, but unaided accuracy fell 15% by week 4 (Rani et al. 2026, small N). We want boosts, not dependence.
- **Our own A/B tests.** Mobile, Shorts, non-English audiences and track-record displays are unstudied. Borrowed US desktop findings are a starting point, not proof.

## 8. The creator side, briefly

The same map serves creators, with the numbers flipped from warning to opportunity:
- Supply per quarter.
- Views per day since publishing.
- Original share.
- The under-covered camp.
- The evidence gap.
- The date of the last original video.

The popularity cues we pull from the viewer's first screen belong here. The framing must stay "what's missing", not "what's hot", or we rebuild the treadmill for creators too.

## 9. Honest limits

- Every individual effect is small. The bet is that ordering several of them at the decision moment adds up. That is a hypothesis to test, not a finding.
- The heavy-viewing minority is resentful and loyal to channels they subscribe to. Neutral maps of camps and claim histories will land better with them than debunks, but no study proves it for our format.
- Track records, which are our most distinctive asset, have never been tested for their effect on trust. We would be generating that evidence ourselves.
