# Research 3 — Interventions that change how people consume and act on content

Researcher brief, 2026-09-15. Citations checked against abstracts or full text.

- **Discernment** means getting better at telling true from false.
- **Scepticism** means doubting everything, true content included.

**Bottom line.** Effects are small: d ≈ 0.1–0.3 in the lab, low single-digit percentages on real platforms, and much smaller in the field than in the lab. Most fade within hours to weeks. What survives gives people skills or context at the moment they need them. What fails hands them verdicts or generic warnings.

## 1. Prebunking and inoculation

**Roozenbeek et al. 2022, *Science Advances***: five 90-second videos teaching manipulation techniques.
- Lab: 6 RCTs, n = 6,464.
  - Technique recognition: d = 0.28–0.68.
  - Trust discernment: d = 0.10–0.32.
  - Sharing discernment: d = 0.10–0.22.
- YouTube field test: pre-roll ads, about 5.4M impressions. 22,632 people answered one item within 24 hours.
  - Effect h = 0.09, about 5% better recognition.
  - It measured recognition only, not viewing or sharing.

**Field caution**: Wang et al. 2025, *PNAS Nexus*. Five preregistered studies, N = 3,881, in a simulated scrolling feed.
- No effect on engagement with real emotional tweets.
- One study with synthetic posts: sharing d = 0.16.
- Lab gains largely disappear in a feed.

**Meta-analyses**
- Banas & Rains 2010, 54 cases: inoculation beats supportive messages and no treatment. It holds at moderate delays and decays after about two weeks.
- Simchon et al. 2025, 33 experiments, N = 37,025: better discrimination with no shift toward calling everything false. Some authors built the interventions.

**Critique: discernment or just scepticism?** Contested; games look the most bias-prone.
- Modirrousta-Galian & Higham 2023 (ROC reanalysis): a shift toward "false" with no discrimination gain.
- Graham et al. 2023, N = 353: more bias, and true content believed less.
- Seabrooke et al. 2025 (preregistered, India): no discrimination gain.

**Durability**
- Maertens et al. 2025, *Nature Communications*, N = 11,759:
  - The game's effect was gone by about day 9.
  - Text held at d ≈ 0.28 at 29 days.
  - Video went from d = 0.295 to d = 0.230 at 29 days (about 78% kept).
- Boosters that review the techniques beat boosters that only warn of the threat.

## 2. Accuracy nudges

**Pennycook et al. 2021, *Nature***: asked 5,379 people who had shared low-quality news on Twitter to rate one headline.
- Over the next 24 hours, the average quality of what they shared rose 4.8%; summed quality rose 9%.

**Pennycook & Rand 2022**: their own meta-analysis of 20 experiments, N = 26,863. Sharing discernment improved, driven by about 10% less sharing of false headlines.

**Replication**
- Roozenbeek et al. 2021 replicated the 2020 COVID study: Stage 1 failed; pooled N = 1,583 gave a small effect.
- Martel et al. 2024, an adversarial collaboration, k = 21, N = 27,828: the nudge works, but weaker for Republicans.

**At scale** (Lin et al. 2024 preprint)
- Facebook, N = 33M: 2.6% fewer people shared misinformation in the following hour.
- Twitter: 3.7–6.3% less low-quality sharing over a week.

**Verdict**: genuine discernment, but small and lasting only hours.

## 3. Lateral reading (civic online reasoning)

**What experts do**
- Wineburg & McGrew 2019: professional fact-checkers leave a site to check who is behind it, and outperform historians and Stanford students. Descriptive study.

**Teaching it**
- Breakstone et al. 2021: after 4 one-hour modules, lateral reading rose from 3% to 77%. No control group.
- Wineburg et al. 2022, *Journal of Educational Psychology*: 271 students vs 228 matched controls (not randomised). Significant gains.
- Axelsson et al. 2021: digital tutorials for 209 Swedish upper-secondary students improved credibility judgements, including for video.
- Panizza et al. 2022: a pop-up prompt increased lateral reading, but accuracy improved only when the source was unfamiliar.

**Tips**
- Guess et al. 2020, *PNAS*: news-literacy tips raised discernment 26.5% in the US and 17.5% in online India, with no effect in rural India. The US effect lasted weeks.
- It also lowered belief in mainstream news, though less.

**Backfire**
- Aslett et al. 2024, *Nature*: telling people to search online *increased* belief in false news, because searches land in data voids.

## 4. Friction and pause prompts

- **Fazio 2020** (N = 501): asking people to explain why a headline is true cut sharing of false headlines with no effect on true ones. That is discernment, but one study and intentions only.
- **Twitter "read before you retweet"** (company data, not peer reviewed): article opens +40%; opening before retweeting +33%.
- **Grüning et al. 2023, *PNAS*** (the one sec app): 36% of app-open attempts abandoned; attempts fell 37% over six weeks. Users chose to install it, so treat this as an upper bound.

## 5. Two-sided and refutational messages

**Allen 1991; O'Keefe 1999**
- Two-sided messages overall: no advantage (r = −.001).
- Two-sided *with* refutation: more persuasive (r = .077) and more credible (r = .117).
- Two-sided *without* refutation: less persuasive (r = −.049).

**False balance**
- Imundo & Rapp 2022: presenting sides as equal distorts beliefs. Statements about the weight of evidence reduce the damage.

**AI dialogues**
- Costello, Pennycook & Rand 2024, *Science*: tailored, evidence-based AI conversations cut conspiracy belief about 20%, still there at 2 months.
- Counterpoint: Rani et al., CHI 2026 (N = 67). AI assistance raised accuracy 21% while it was available, but accuracy without it fell 15.3% by week 4.

## 6. Consider the opposite, open-minded thinking, intellectual humility

- **Lord, Lepper & Preston 1984**: "consider the opposite" beat "be fair" at reducing biased judgement. Small student samples from before the replication crisis.
- **Biddlestone et al. 2025**: interventions raised actively open-minded thinking; discernment improved only indirectly, through that change.
- **Porter & Schumann 2018**: a growth-mindset reading raised intellectual humility.
- None of these has a field outcome on behaviour. Promising only.

## 7. Labels, warnings and Community Notes

**The implied truth effect**: Pennycook, Bear, Collins & Rand 2020, N = 6,739.
- False headlines with a warning tag were judged accurate 14.5% of the time, down from 17%.
- *Untagged* false headlines rose to 19%.
- Marking true headlines as verified helped.

**Warning wording**
- Clayton et al. 2020: "Rated false" beats "Disputed". Generic warnings also lower belief in true news.

**Source ratings**
- Aslett et al. 2022 (NewsGuard labels, field, N = 3,337): no average effect. The heaviest misinformation consumers did improve.
- Kim, Moravec & Dennis 2019: source ratings change belief; expert-based ratings are strongest.

**Community Notes**
- Slaughter et al. 2025, *PNAS* (40,078 posts): once a note is attached, reposts fall 46% and likes 44%.
- Over a post's whole lifetime reposts fall only 11.6%, because notes arrive late.
- Chuai et al. 2024: notes come too slowly to matter much.

## 8. Boosts vs nudges, and publication bias

- **Hertwig & Grüne-Yanoff 2017**: boosts build lasting skills; nudges work only while they're in place.
- **Mertens et al. 2022**: average nudge effect d = 0.43.
- **Maier et al. 2022**, corrected for publication bias:
  - Overall d = 0.04.
  - Information nudges d = 0.00, with strong evidence of no effect (BF₀₁ ≈ 34).
  - Structural nudges such as defaults d = 0.12, undecided.
- **Szaszi et al. 2022**: effects vary so widely that averages mislead.
- **DellaVigna & Linos 2022**: 8.7 percentage points in published papers vs 1.4 in government nudge units at scale.

## 9. Self-regulation tools

**Allcott, Gentzkow & Song 2022, *AER*** (about 2,000 users; YouTube among the tracked apps)
- Self-set limits cut use by 22 minutes a day (16%). 78% chose binding limits.
- A temporary payment cut 56 minutes a day, and use stayed lower after it ended.
- Their model attributes 31% of social media use to self-control problems.

**Allcott et al. 2020, *AER*** (four weeks without Facebook)
- Well-being +0.09 SD and less polarisation.
- News knowledge fell.
- Use stayed lower afterwards.

## 10. Prediction, accountability and track records

**Accountability**
- Lerner & Tetlock 1999: accountability improves thinking only when it comes before people form a view, to an audience whose views are unknown.
- A known audience produces conformity; accountability after the fact produces defensiveness.

**Forecasting training**
- Mellers et al. 2014; Chang et al. 2016: under an hour of training improved forecast accuracy (Brier score) by 6–11%.

**Questions before a video**
- Carpenter & Toftness 2017: questions before short videos improved learning.
- Toftness et al. 2018: limited effects with lecture-length videos.
- Motz et al. 2026 (ManyClasses 2): benefits on average across real classes, but they depend on prior knowledge, and some low-knowledge students disengaged.

**Showing a source's prediction track record**
- No rigorous experiment shows it changes viewer trust or behaviour. The closest evidence is Kim et al. 2019 (lab) and NewsGuard (no average effect).
- **Narrative Radar would be testing something new.**

## Interventions worth building, ranked by evidence

1. **Rebuttal-first claim cards with weight of evidence.** Each claim shows the best counter-argument, the evidence, and each camp's support. Camps are never equal columns. Shown during viewing, not after.
2. **Technique prebunks with spaced boosters.** A 60–90 second card naming the tactic in this video ("false dichotomy at 4:12"), then a review quiz about 10 days later. Video or text, not a game.
3. **Curated lateral reading.** One tap to see who this channel is and who covered the story first, drawn from vetted sources. Never "search it yourself".
4. **Explain before you share or act.** "How do you know this is true?" when someone shares, saves or acts on a video. Single-digit percentage effects lasting hours, but cheap, and it improves discernment.
5. **Labels on everything.** Every claim carries a status: verified, contested, or not yet checked. Nothing left unlabelled, and no generic warning banners.
6. **Predict before watching, then show the receipts.** Promising; needs our own A/B test. Optional, because low-knowledge users may disengage.
7. **Self-set limits,** opt-in only. "You've watched this claim recycled 6 times this week."
8. **Channel track-record card.** Unproven; run a randomised test before claiming any impact.

## Looks good but backfires or doesn't replicate

- **Games as the main tool** (Bad News): mostly generic scepticism, gone in about 9 days.
- **Generic misinformation warnings:** lower belief in true news too.
- **Labelling only some claims:** makes the unlabelled false ones more believable (implied truth effect).
- **"Search it yourself":** increases belief in false news (Aslett 2024).
- **Both sides shown without refutation, or as equals:** less persuasive, and creates false balance.
- **Raw exposure to the other camp:** Bail et al. 2018 — Republicans who followed a liberal bot became more conservative.
- **Correcting users publicly:** they then shared lower-quality, more partisan, more toxic content (Mosleh et al. 2021).
- **Source badges on their own:** no average effect (Aslett 2022).
- **Information-only nudges:** d ≈ 0 after bias correction. Field effects are about a sixth of lab effects.
- **Lab inoculation gains in realistic feeds:** largely vanish (Wang 2025).
- **AI verdicts that do the thinking:** people build no lasting skill (Rani 2026, small N).
- **Fear of the backfire effect:** overstated. Wood & Porter 2019 found none across 52 issues with 10,100 people. Corrections are safe to show.
