# Research 2 — Why one video persuades and why people keep watching

Researcher brief, 2026-09-15. Citations verified. Evidence types: META = meta-analysis; RCT/FIELD = randomised trial; LAB = lab experiment; OBS = observational or self-report.

## 1. Illusory truth: repetition raises belief

**Evidence: strong.**

- **Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino 1977.** Statements repeated across sessions two weeks apart were rated more valid.
- **Dechêne et al. 2010** (META, 51 studies). d = 0.39 when the same item is rated again; d = 0.50 for repeated vs new items.
- **Fazio et al. 2015.** Repetition raised belief even in statements that contradict what people already know.
- **Pennycook, Cannon & Rand 2018.** A single exposure to a real fake headline raised perceived accuracy. The effect lasted a week and survived a "disputed" tag.
- **Hassan & Barber 2021.** Tested up to 27 repetitions. The increase is logarithmic: the second exposure adds the most.
- **Replication:** robust. Each single exposure has only a modest effect.
- **On YouTube:** recycled uploads and reaction videos deliver those early repeat exposures. The same claim from many channels feels corroborated even when it traces back to one source.
- **Countermeasure:** Brashier, Eliseev & Marsh 2020 (*Cognition*). Asking people to judge accuracy on first exposure prevented the later illusion, but only when they had relevant knowledge.

## 2. Continued influence of misinformation

**Evidence: strong.**

- **Lewandowsky et al. 2012; Ecker et al. 2022** (reviews). Retracted claims keep shaping reasoning. Corrections work best when they offer an alternative explanation.
- **Chan, Jones, Hall Jamieson & Albarracín 2017** (META, k = 52, N = 6,878).
  - Misinformation effect: d = 2.41–3.08. Debunking effect: d = 1.14–1.33. Belief that persisted after debunking: d = 0.75–1.06.
  - Detailed debunks worked better than brief ones.
  - Belief persisted more when people had generated their own reasons for it.
- **Walter & Tukachinsky 2020** (META, 32 studies).
  - Corrections were weaker when the misinformation came from a credible source, had been repeated, or the correction arrived late.
  - They were stronger when the original source issued the correction.
- **On YouTube:** a debunk that arrives weeks later, from a different channel, after many re-uploads, meets all three weakening conditions.

## 3. Curiosity and the information gap

**Evidence: moderate.** The mechanism holds; the clickbait effects are mixed.

- **Loewenstein 1994** (theory). Curiosity is the deprivation felt when a gap in knowledge is noticed.
- **Kang et al. 2009** (fMRI plus behaviour). Curiosity recruits reward circuitry, and people spend tokens or time to resolve it.
- **Hsee & Ruan 2016** (4 LAB experiments). Curiosity drives people to seek out aversive outcomes, including electric shocks.
- **Clickbait studies conflict.** Molyneux & Coddington 2020 say clickbait "may lower" credibility. Munger et al. 2020 (*POQ*) found no effect on polarisation, retention or media trust.
- **Gap:** no rigorous study of how satisfied people feel after clicking clickbait.
- **On YouTube:** "what they're not telling you" titles and end-of-video teasers open a gap that only the next video closes.

## 4. Negativity, fear and doomscrolling

**Evidence: strong for clicks and attention; weak for doomscrolling as a cause of harm.**

- **Robertson et al. 2023** (*Nature Human Behaviour*; 22,743 randomised Upworthy headline tests, about 370M impressions).
  - Each negative word raised click-through by about 2.3%; each positive word lowered it by about 1.0%.
  - A 2025 reanalysis confirmed the negative effect but not the positive one.
- **Soroka, Fournier & Nir 2019** (*PNAS*; 17 countries). Negative video news is more physiologically arousing, with large differences between individuals. A 2024 Dutch replication found only "suggestive" support.
- **Brady et al. 2017; Brady et al. 2025** (*PNAS Nexus*; preregistered replication plus META of 27 studies). Moral-emotional language raised sharing by 13% per word (IRR 1.13).
- **Tannenbaum et al. 2015** (META, 127 studies). Fear appeals change attitudes and behaviour, d = 0.29.
- **Doomscrolling research** is mostly cross-sectional self-report.
  - The measurement scales: Sharma, Lee & Johnson 2022; Satici et al. 2022, the paper actually titled "Doomscrolling Scale".
  - Price et al. 2022 ran a 30-day diary study (observational).
  - Buchanan et al. 2021 (experiment): 2–4 minutes of COVID news lowered mood and optimism.

## 5. Authority and parasocial bonds

**Evidence: moderate, mostly correlational.**

- **Pornpitakpan 2004** (review). High-credibility sources are usually more persuasive, though it depends on conditions.
- **Tukachinsky, Walter & Saucier 2020** (META, k = 120). Parasocial relationships are strongly linked to perceived similarity, identification and persuasion. They are not linked to loneliness.
- **Sokolova & Kefi 2020** (survey). Parasocial interaction predicted purchase intention more strongly than credibility did.
- **Caveat:** these are mostly surveys, so cause and effect is not established.
- **On YouTube:** a creator watched for hundreds of hours works like a trusted friend. Misinformation from credible sources is also harder to correct (section 2).

## 6. Single source, overconfidence

**Evidence: mixed.**

- **Rozenblit & Keil 2002.** People overrate how well they understand mechanisms until they have to explain them (the illusion of explanatory depth). Replicated, including preregistered studies in *JDM* 2023.
- **Fernbach et al. 2013.** Explaining how a policy works lowered felt understanding and extremity.
  - **Partly failed to replicate.** Crawford & Ruscio 2021 ran three preregistered studies: felt understanding fell, but extremity did not.
  - Sloman & Vives 2022: the effect appears only on consequentialist issues, not protected-value ones.
- **Brenner, Koehler & Tversky 1996** (LAB).
  - People who saw only one side of a legal case leaned toward it and were more confident and less accurate, even when explicitly told the other side existed.
  - Asking them to estimate how strong the other side's case was "greatly reduced" the bias.
  - Small samples; no modern large replication found.

## 7. Identity-protective / motivated reasoning

**Evidence: the bias is real, but the "smarter people are more biased" version is oversold.**

- **Kahan et al. 2017.** More numerate people interpreted data more ideologically.
- **Ditto et al. 2019** (META, 51 studies, N > 18,000). Partisan bias r = .245, the same on left and right.
- **Failed or undermined:**
  - Persson et al. 2021 (preregistered) failed to replicate the numeracy finding.
  - Stagnaro, Tappin & Rand 2023 (*PNAS*, probability sample) also failed to replicate it.
  - Tappin, Pennycook & Rand 2021 raised problems with the causal inference.

## 8. Autoplay, defaults and no stopping point

**Evidence: moderate. Samples are small, except the economics RCT.**

- **Schaffner et al. 2025** (CSCW; 76-person Netflix field experiment). Turning autoplay off cut viewing by about 21 minutes a day and about 18 minutes per session.
- **Lukoff et al. 2021** (CHI; 120 users). On YouTube, autoplay and recommendations erode users' sense of agency; search and playlists support it.
- **Allcott, Gentzkow & Song 2022** (*AER*, RCT). Social media use is habit-forming, and self-control problems account for about 31% of it.
- **Variable rewards:** Lindström et al. 2021 found reinforcement-learning patterns in posting (observational). Not rigorously tested for watching.
- **Wansink's "bottomless bowl"** is excluded: 18 retractions and a misconduct finding.

## 9. Availability cascades and social proof

**Evidence: cascades are theory only; popularity-cue experiments are solid.**

- **Kuran & Sunstein 1999.** Conceptual argument: a belief spreads because its public visibility makes it seem plausible. Not tested directly.
- **Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006** (*Science*, N = 14,341). Showing download counts made song success more unequal and less predictable.
- **Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013** (*Science*; field RCT, more than 100,000 comments). One planted upvote made a positive rating 32% more likely and raised final scores by 25%. Planted downvotes were corrected by other users.
- **Avram et al. 2020** (HKS Misinformation Review, 8,606 players). High engagement numbers made players like and share low-credibility articles more and fact-check them less.
- **Gap:** no experiment isolates the effect of YouTube view counts on belief.

## Mechanisms we can realistically counter, ranked by evidence

1. **Illusory truth from recycled videos (META).** Show "first appeared [date] by [channel], repeated in N videos", so repetition reads as one source. Prompt an accuracy judgement on first exposure (Brashier 2020).
2. **Continued influence (META).** Put detailed, alternative-explanation corrections next to the claim, before repetition builds up and without delay.
3. **Popularity cues (field RCTs).** Show evidence status next to the view count. "2M views" is not verification.
4. **Fear and outrage framing (large click data plus META).** Name the technique on the card. Prebunking against techniques worked in field trials run as YouTube ads (Roozenbeek et al. 2022).
5. **One-sided overconfidence (LAB).** Show each camp's strongest case and ask "how strong is the other side?", the manipulation that worked in Brenner et al. 1996.
6. **No stopping point (field experiment and RCT).** Give the page an end: "you've now seen the whole narrative: 4 camps, 12 claims".
7. **Illusion of understanding (LAB, mixed).** "Explain how X causes Y" prompts reliably reduce felt understanding. Don't promise moderation.
8. **Authority and parasocial trust (correlational).** A track record of the creator's past predictions is plausible but untested.

## "Attention hacking" claims weaker than commonly believed

- **The backfire effect.** Wood & Porter 2019 tested 52 issues with more than 10,100 people and found no backfire.
- **The algorithmic rabbit hole.**
  - Hosseinmardi et al. 2021 found little evidence that recommendations drive radical viewing.
  - Chen et al. 2023: extremist viewing comes mostly through subscriptions and outside links, by a small, already resentful group.
  - Liu et al. 2025 (*PNAS*, about 9,000 people): filter-bubble recommendations had limited effects on opinions.
- **"Smarter people are more biased"** (motivated numeracy). Failed preregistered replications.
- **"Explaining mechanisms moderates extremists."** The moderation part failed to replicate.
- **"Clickbait destroys trust."** Mixed findings, including a null result.
- **"Doomscrolling causes mental illness."** Evidence is mostly cross-sectional.
- **"Feeds are dopamine slot machines."** Untested for watching.
- **The 8-second "goldfish" attention span.** No traceable source.
- **Bottomless-bowl stopping cues.** Wansink misconduct.
