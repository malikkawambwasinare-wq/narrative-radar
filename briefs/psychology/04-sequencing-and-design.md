# Research 4 — Order and form: building the map before the persuasion

Researcher brief, 2026-09-15. Citations checked against publisher pages or abstract indexes. Effect sizes seen only via secondary sources, and findings that failed or are contested, are flagged inline.

Evidence strength scale: **Strong** = meta-analysis or large confirmatory replication. **Moderate** = several experiments. **Weak** = one study, lab-only, or theory.

## 1. Order effects

**Belief-adjustment model** — Hogarth & Einhorn 1992, *Cognitive Psychology* 24:1–55 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/001002859290002J)
- Judging after each item favours whatever came last.
- Judging once at the end, or after long series of ~17+ items, favours whatever came first. People commit, then discount later evidence.
- Strength: Moderate. Five experiments, N = 192; many replications in accounting, some inconsistent.

**When the last message wins** — Miller & Campbell 1959 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13664403/)
- The more recent message wins when there's a delay between messages and the judgment comes right after the second.
- Only partially replicated (Insko 1964).

**Involvement decides it** — Haugtvedt & Wegener 1994, *J Consumer Research* 21:205–218 (https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/21/1/205/1853761)
- High involvement: the first message wins. Low involvement: the last one wins.
- So engaged viewers are the ones most anchored by the first source they see.

**Anchoring**
- Replicated with large effects in Many Labs (Klein et al. 2014).

**Misinformation gets a first-exposure advantage**
- Walter & Tukachinsky 2020 meta-analysis, 32 studies (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0093650219854600): corrections don't fully undo it (r = −.05). They work worse when the misinformation was repeated, came from a credible source, or the correction arrived late.
- Pennycook, Cannon & Rand 2018 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30247057/): a single exposure raises perceived accuracy, even when the headline carries a "disputed" tag.
- Brashier et al. 2021, *PNAS*, N = 2,683 (https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2020043118): fact-checks shown *after* a headline improved truth discernment a week later more than checks shown during or before.

**Flag:** the "backfire effect" doesn't reliably replicate (Swire-Thompson et al. 2020).

**What this means for the page**
- Nothing persuasive goes first: no autoplaying video, and the creator's framing isn't the hero.
- Open with neutral structure.
- Put claim-level verdicts right after the viewer meets a claim, not long before.
- Repeat claim wording as little as possible.

## 2. Map before territory (organizers, pre-training, signaling)

**Advance organizers** — Luiten, Ames & Ackerson 1980 meta-analysis, 135 studies (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/00028312017002211)
- Small gains: ES ≈ 0.21 immediate, 0.26 retention.
- Mayer 1979 argued the effect is conditional, mostly on unfamiliar material.

**Pre-training** — Mayer, Mathias & Wetzell 2002, *JEP: Applied* 8:147–154 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12240927/)
- Teaching the names and features of key components *before* the lesson improved transfer in all 3 experiments. Teaching them afterwards did not help.
- Mayer's handbook tally gives a median d ≈ 0.75. That's his own count, not an independent meta-analysis, and was confirmed only via secondary sources.

**Signaling** — Schneider et al. 2018 meta-analysis, 103 studies, N = 12,201 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X17300581)
- g = 0.53 for retention, 0.33 for transfer, and lower cognitive load.
- Strength: Strong.

**What this means for the page**
- Before play, show a compact map: 3–5 named claims, 2–4 camps, and where this video sits.
- Name things before giving details.
- Keep it short. Gains for critical evaluation, as opposed to recall, are mostly assumed rather than shown.

## 3. Prequestions: predict before you watch

**Early findings**
- Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19751074/): answering prequestions helps later learning, even when the answer given was wrong, as long as the right answer is then studied.
- Carpenter & Toftness 2017 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2211368116301103): prequestions before a short video helped both the asked content and content that wasn't asked about.

**Failed to generalise**
- In authentic lectures over 20 minutes, the benefit to non-asked content was absent or marginal (Toftness et al. 2018).

**Meta-analyses**
- Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 2023, preregistered (https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-023-02353-8): g = 0.54 for asked items, g = 0.04 for everything else.
- 2025 multilevel meta-analysis (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10075-7): g = .66 vs .01.
- Pan & Carpenter 2023 review.
- Strength: Strong, for the asked items only.

**What this means for the page**
- Ask 1–2 prequestions aimed at exactly what matters, e.g. "When do you think this claim first appeared?" or "Did past predictions like this come true?"
- Reveal the answer. Only the asked items get the boost.

## 4. Single stories vs patterns (episodic vs thematic framing)

- **Iyengar 1991.** Stories about a single case (episodic) push blame onto individuals; coverage of the pattern (thematic) pushes it onto systems. Replication is conditional: Boukes 2022 found the effects depend on ideology and country.
- **Aarøe 2011** (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10584609.2011.568041). Pattern framing wins when emotion is low. Single-case stories win when emotion is intense.

**What this means for the page**
- Lead with the pattern: "this video is one instance of narrative X".
- Keep the page's emotional temperature low. The video itself will be the emotional single case.

## 5. Base rates, denominators and vivid cases

**Natural frequencies beat percentages**
- Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995.
- McDowell & Jacobs 2017 meta-analysis: correct reasoning rose from ~4% to ~24% when problems were stated as natural frequencies.
- Strength: Strong.

**Vivid examples distort, even beside the numbers**
- Gibson & Zillmann 1994: exaggerated exemplars distorted how serious readers thought an issue was, even with precise base rates shown, and the effect lasted.
- Identifiable-victim effect: real but small, r = .05 across 41 studies (Lee & Feeley 2016).
- Small, Loewenstein & Slovic 2007: teaching people about this bias made them give less to the individual victim without raising giving to the statistical ones.

**What this means for the page**
- Use counts like "1 of 33 videos", "claim first seen March 2021", "3 of 14 dated predictions came true", not percentages or scores.
- Never give the source video visual weight beyond its share of the narrative.
- Don't lecture viewers about bias.

## 6. Two-sided presentation and false balance

**Two-sided messages**
- Allen 1991; O'Keefe 1999: two-sided messages that *refute* the other side beat one-sided ones (r ≈ .08).
- Two-sided messages that *don't* refute are *less* persuasive than one-sided ones.

**False balance**
- Dixon & Clarke 2013, N = 327: balanced vaccine–autism coverage reduced certainty and made experts look divided.
- Koehler 2016, *JEP: Applied* 22:24–38: one quote per side blurred high- vs low-consensus issues, even when the expert panel count was shown.
- Imundo & Rapp 2022: weight-of-evidence statements reduce the harm but don't eliminate it.
- Strength: Moderate, consistent.

**Stance labels** — Gao et al. 2018, CSCW
- Labelling each source's stance increased selective exposure and made fake articles seem more trustworthy.

**What this means for the page**
- Size camps by weight of evidence and track record, never by video count or airtime.
- Never show symmetric side-by-side cards.
- Put the weight-of-evidence statement *before* the camp cards.

## 7. What the viewer does next

**Autoplay**
- Schaffner et al. 2025, CSCW, Netflix field experiment, n = 76, 10–17 days: turning autoplay off cut viewing by 21 minutes a day and sessions by 17.6 minutes, and added ~24 seconds between episodes. Strength: Moderate (small sample).
- Hiniker et al. 2018, CHI: autoplay after planned content eroded preschoolers' self-set limits.
- Lukoff et al. 2021, CHI: autoplay and recommendations undermine users' sense of agency; playlists and search support it.

**Stopping points**
- Cheema & Soman 2008, JMR: partitions create decision points and reduce consumption.
- Rixen et al. 2023: people mostly stop scrolling for external reasons, because infinite feeds give no internal stopping cues.

**Balance feedback** — Munson, Lee & Resnick 2013
- Feedback on reading balance shifted behaviour modestly: 1–2 more visits a week to opposing sites.

**"Are you still watching?" prompts**
- No peer-reviewed evaluation found.

**What this means for the page**
- Give a finite set ("4 videos cover the whole narrative") with a progress marker and an explicit "you've seen every camp" endpoint.
- No autoplay.

## 8. Curiosity used for good

**Research**
- Loewenstein 1994: curiosity is the discomfort of a known gap in knowledge.
- Kang et al. 2009: higher curiosity goes with better memory for surprising answers.
- Gruber et al. 2014, *Neuron*: curiosity also boosts memory for unrelated material learned at the same time; Stare et al. 2018 found this persists.

**Headlines**
- Aubin Le Quéré & Matias 2025, *Scientific Reports*, 27,616 Upworthy headline A/B tests: clicks peak at intermediate concreteness; headlines too vague or too specific both lose. Engagement after the click wasn't measured.
- Molyneux & Coddington 2020: clickbait may lower perceived credibility.

**What this means for the page**
- Open a concrete, answerable gap ("This claim predates the video by 3 years. Where did it start?") and close it on the page.
- Avoid vague teasers.

## 9. Visual summaries and consensus displays

**Consensus messaging**
- van Stekelenburg et al. 2022, preregistered meta-analysis of 43 experiments: perceived consensus rose g = 0.55, but factual belief only g = 0.12. Preregistered studies showed smaller effects.
- The "gateway belief model" replicated with N = 6,301 but is disputed (Kahan 2017).
- Reactance was reported by Ma et al. 2019; later replications contested it.

**Formats**
- van der Linden et al. 2014: a simple text statement or pie chart beat metaphors, including across party lines.

**Gaps**
- No strong randomized evidence that timelines or stance maps improve understanding of contested claims. NewsCube (CHI 2009) only had small user studies.
- A consensus meter only applies where real expert consensus exists. Most YouTube narratives (market calls, political forecasts) don't have one, so show track records instead.

## A research-backed sequence for a narrative page

Everything before step 6 happens before play.

**1. Pattern headline, no video yet.** "This video is 1 of 33 in narrative X, which first appeared in [date]."
- Evidence: thematic framing (§4), natural frequencies (§5), first-message advantage for involved viewers (§1).
- Risk: dismissed as spin. Keep it descriptive.

**2. One prediction prompt.** "When do you think this claim first appeared?" Answer revealed immediately.
- Evidence: prequestions help the asked items, g ≈ 0.54 (§3); a closable curiosity gap (§8).
- Risk: friction and drop-off. Make it one tap and skippable.

**3. The map.** Named claims (3–5) and camps, with this video highlighted.
- Evidence: pre-training and organizers (§2); signaling, g = 0.53 (§2).
- Risk: overload. Hard cap, progressive disclosure.

**4. Weight of evidence and track records, before camp arguments.** "Dated predictions from this camp: 3 of 14 came true."
- Evidence: weight-of-evidence statements cut false balance (§6); natural frequencies (§5).
- Risks: reactance (contested); unreliable track-record scoring would destroy trust.

**5. Camp cards sized by evidence.** Each with its strongest argument and its refutation.
- Evidence: refutational two-sided messages (§6).
- Risks: false balance; stance labels driving selective exposure (Gao 2018).

**6. Play, with claim-level context pinned to timestamps.** "This claim: first seen 2021; 2 of 9 similar forecasts held."
- Evidence: corrections placed after exposure work best (§1).
- Risk: repeating claim text builds familiarity (§1).

**7. A finite exit.** "You've now seen 3 of 3 camps." Offer 1–3 curated next videos, no autoplay, and an explicit stopping point.
- Evidence: autoplay RCT and agency research (§7); partitions (§7).
- Risk: lower engagement numbers. Measure understanding, not minutes.

## What NOT to do

- Don't load or autoplay the video, its thumbnail or its title first.
- Don't show "Side A vs Side B" as equal cards, and don't size camps by video count or views.
- Don't use percentages, trust scores or opaque meters where counts work.
- Don't restate false claims repeatedly or in bold headlines. Summarise once, next to their record.
- Don't use vague curiosity teasers or confrontational "you've been fooled" copy.
- Don't show consensus meters for topics without real expert consensus.
- Don't rely on structure alone. Kotz et al. 2023, N = 4,906: the "truth sandwich" format did no better than other structures; the content does the work.
- Don't use infinite related-video rails.

## Verification notes

- Confirmed only via secondary summaries: Mayer's d ≈ 0.75 tally, and the .66/.01 figures from the 2025 meta-analysis.
- Failed or contested, and flagged above: the backfire effect, the gateway belief model (Kahan), consensus reactance, prequestion benefits for non-asked content in long videos, and discernment gains from gamified inoculation (Modirrousta-Galian & Higham 2023).
